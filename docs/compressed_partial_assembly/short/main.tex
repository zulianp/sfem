\begin{abstract}
Newton--Krylov solvers for finite-strain elasticity spend most of their time applying the tangent
operator, and how that operator is stored decides both the time and the memory of a solve.  The
assembled matrix has the largest memory footprint and is limited by memory bandwidth; matrix-free
application stores no tangent but is limited by arithmetic.  A compressed tangent reduces both the
footprint and the bytes each application streams, but its encoding perturbs the operator, and the
effect on the Newton and Krylov iteration counts must be assessed with the speed-up.  We develop
three compressed formats across this range: the quantized block sparse (BSR) matrix, the element
matrix quantized per node pair, and the quadrature-point store, which keeps the pulled-back material
tangent at the quadrature points or, on quadratic tetrahedra, the ten weighted sums of it that the
application uses.  They share \texttt{fp16}, \texttt{int8} and \texttt{int8} mean-plus-deviation
encodings decoded inside the application, and the matrix formats a difference form that keeps rigid
translations exactly in the kernel of the encoded operator.  We show that an encoding error enters
inexact Newton as an additive forcing term.  On linear and quadratic tetrahedra and trilinear and
triquadratic hexahedra at $10^7$ degrees of freedom, half precision and \texttt{int8} mean plus
deviation keep the exact Newton iteration counts.  On a 72-core Arm Neoverse-V2 CPU the quantized
BSR matrix is the fastest format on tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra, solving in
$\timePctQbsrEightHexEight$--$\timePctQbsrHalfTetTen\%$ of the time of single-precision BSR from
$\QbsrHalfMemVsThirtyTwoTetTen$--$\QbsrEightMemVsThirtyTwoHexEight\times$ less operator memory, and
the element matrix on triquadratic hexahedra, solving in $\timePctEaDiffHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ of that time.  On one H100 GPU,
against our tuned BSR product $\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMin$--$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMax\times$ faster than
cuSPARSE's for our problems of interest, the quantized matrix is the fastest on linear tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra,
solving in $\gpuTenmTimePctQbsrHalfTetFour\%$ and $\gpuTenmTimePctQbsrEightHexEight\%$ of the time of single-precision BSR, and the
quadrature-point stores on the quadratic elements, solving in $\gpuTenmTimePctQpEightMdTetTen\%$ and
$\gpuTenmTimePctQpHalfHexTwentySeven\%$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Newton--Krylov solvers for finite-strain elasticity apply the tangent operator tens to hundreds of
times per Newton step.  Three families of formats compete for that operator.  An assembled matrix,
in block sparse row (BSR) form for a vector problem, pays one assembly per step and then applies at
the speed of memory: our BSR product in double precision sustains $\MachineBandwidth$~GB/s on the
Grace CPU, $\MachineBandwidthPct\%$ of its nominal $\MachineNominalBandwidth$~GB/s, and
$\gpuBsrSixtyFourGbsMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourGbsMax$~GB/s on the H100 GPU,
$\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMax\%$ of its nominal $\gpuNominalBandwidth$~GB/s.  Element matrices store each element's dense block and apply it
with a gather and a scatter.  Partial assembly stores less --- the material tangent at the
quadrature points --- and applies it by a contraction with the reference element; at the far end, a matrix-free application re-evaluates the constitutive law every time.
Moving along this range trades stored bytes against arithmetic, and quantizing the stored numbers
shifts the trade for every format at once.

The question this paper answers is which format, with which encoding, gives the shortest time to
solution and the smallest memory for a Newton--Krylov hyperelasticity solve, and why.  The answer
depends on three things the formats share: the bytes each streams per degree of freedom, the
arithmetic and gathering each performs, and the perturbation each encoding makes to the tangent,
which the Newton iteration either absorbs or pays for in iterations.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a common framework for the formats: each is a store written once per Newton step and an
  application that decodes it in registers, under the same \texttt{fp16} and \texttt{int8}
  encodings, with an error model in which what matters is the absolute rounding on smooth, nearly
  rigid modes, and its consequence for inexact Newton --- the encoding error adds to the forcing
  term;
\item the difference form, which stores only off-diagonal node blocks and keeps rigid translations
  exactly in the kernel of an encoded element or assembled matrix, with a scale per node pair for
  element matrices and, for the assembled matrix, each pair of blocks stored once in a
  SELL-C-$\sigma$ layout applied without atomics;
\item an evaluation on one Grace CPU and one H100 GPU for four element types up to $10^7$ degrees of
  freedom: throughput, operator and peak memory, Newton iteration counts and time to solution against
  BSR in double and single precision, and the resulting choice of format per element and machine.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Storing geometric factors at quadrature points and applying an operator by contraction underlies
the matrix-free frameworks of deal.II~\cite{KronbichlerKormann2012}, MFEM~\cite{MFEM2021} and
libCEED~\cite{libCEED2021}.  For finite-strain hyperelasticity, Davydov et
al.~\cite{Davydov2020} compare caching levels up to the stress with the full spatial tangent and
find them faster than a sparse matrix above polynomial degree one; Brown et al.~\cite{Brown2022}
store $17$ to $31$ scalars per point; Schussnig et al.~\cite{Schussnig2025} and Wichrowski et
al.~\cite{Wichrowski2025} extend the storage levels to anisotropic and automatically differentiated
materials.  All store double-precision point values.  Storage precision decoupled from arithmetic
precision is established in sparse linear algebra~\cite{Anzt2019,Gruetzmacher2023,HighamMary2022};
for sparse matrix--vector products the precision has been chosen per non-zero~\cite{Ahmad2019} or
per row~\cite{Tezcan2022} among floating-point formats, and matrix-free multigrid runs in single
precision inside a double-precision iteration~\cite{KronbichlerLjungkvist2019}.  Fixed-rate
compression~\cite{Lindstrom2014} and microscaling formats~\cite{Rouhani2023} share a scale per small
block.  Adaptive-precision products store the smallest entries of a sparse matrix in as few as
eight bits~\cite{Graillat2024}; finite-element solvers have run their products in mixed $16$- to
$64$-bit formats~\cite{Ichimura2018} and on INT8 tensor cores with an exactly represented element
matrix~\cite{Ichimura2024}, and computed element matrices in mixed single and half
precision~\cite{CrociWells2024}.  We found no comparison of reduced-precision formats across the
assembled, element and quadrature levels, no stiffness matrix with every entry encoded in eight
bits, and no encoding that keeps the rigid translations in the kernel of the encoded operator,
although the difference form itself is that of edge-based schemes~\cite{Lohner2008}.  Inexact Newton methods converge at a rate set
by the forcing term~\cite{DemboEisenstatSteihaug1982}, to which a Jacobian error
adds~\cite{Kelley2018,Catinas2001}; Jacobians stored in reduced precision have been analysed in this
way~\cite{Kelley2022}.

\section{Formats}

All formats represent the same element action.  On an element with reference basis functions
$\varphi_p$ (one per node $p$), quadrature points $\xhat_q$ with weights $w_q$ and $J$ the Jacobian of
the map from the reference element, let $A_{ijkl}=\partial P_{ij}/\partial F_{kl}$ be the hyperelastic
tangent, the derivative of the first Piola--Kirchhoff stress $P$ with respect to the deformation
gradient $F$~\cite{BonetWood2008}.  Pulled back to the reference element with the weight folded in,
it is $\Sq_{ikmn} = w_q\sum_{j,l} A_{ijkl}\,J^{-1}_{nj} J^{-1}_{ml}\det J$ at $\xhat_q$, and the tangent
operator $\mathcal{H}$ applied to an increment $h$ gives at node $p$, component $i$,
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{ip} = \sum_{q}\sum_{k,m,n} \Sq_{ikmn}\, \partial_m h_k(\xhat_q)\,
  \partial_n\varphi_p(\xhat_q) ,
  \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
with $i,k$ the displacement components and $m,n$ the reference coordinates.
The formats differ in how much of \eqref{eq:action} is precomputed once per Newton step and stored:
the tangent at the points (partial assembly), the element matrix it produces, or the assembled
matrix.

\subsection{Quadrature-point stores}

Everything in \eqref{eq:action} except $\Sq$ belongs to the reference element; with the major
symmetry of an energy each point carries $45$ numbers.  The tangent enters only through the products
$\partial_m\varphi_j\,\partial_n\varphi_p$ of reference gradients, so the application sees only its
weighted sums against them, its moments against the space $\Pi_{mn}$ these products span: as many
numbers per component as the rank $\rho_{mn}$ of the matrix of the products' values at the points,
at most $\min(n_q,\dim\Pi_{mn})$, reproduce \eqref{eq:action} exactly.  Table~\ref{tab:stores} lists
$r$, the dimension of the space the reference derivatives span, which fixes $\dim\Pi_{mn}$, and
$\rho_{mn}$, which sets the count.  On quadratic tetrahedra
(TET10) the gradients are linear and their products quadratic, so the ten moments
$\Mab=\sum_q\lambda_a(\xhat_q)\lambda_b(\xhat_q)\Sq$ against products of the barycentric coordinates
$\lambda_a$ replace the eleven points of the degree-$4$ rule~\cite{Keast1986}.  On trilinear (HEX8) and
triquadratic (HEX27) hexahedra the components with $m=n$ need fewer numbers than points, since their
products do not vary, or vary quadratically, along $\xi_m$: one point per line of points in
direction $m$ passes its $m=n$ components to the line's other points, weighted by their Lagrange
polynomials at it, and the application skips the dropped terms (Figure~\ref{fig:fmtpoints}, bottom).  The folded
stores hold the minima,
$\minimalHexEight$ and $\minimalHexTwentySeven$ numbers instead of $\perpointHexEight$ and
$\perpointHexTwentySeven$ point values, exactly, and apply faster on Grace for every encoding
(\texttt{fp16} by $\foldGainHalfHexEight\%$ on HEX8, $\foldGainHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ on HEX27).
The store is laid out per
block of $V$ elements, one per vector lane, ordered point, component, lane
(Figure~\ref{fig:fmtpoints}).

\subsection{Element matrices in difference form}

Contracting the stored tensors with the basis derivatives once per Newton step gives the
$3N\times3N$ matrix of an element of $N$ nodes, directly and without probing.  An element matrix
annihilates rigid translations, so each diagonal node block is minus the sum of its row's
off-diagonal blocks.  Storing only the $3\times3$ blocks $K_{pq}$ that couple nodes $p<q$, and applying
$y_p=\sum_{q\ne p}K_{pq}(h_q-h_p)$, with $h_q$ the increment at node $q$ and $y_p$ the result at node
$p$, keeps the translations in the kernel of the \emph{encoded} matrix whatever the rounding.  One
\texttt{int8} scale per element spends its range on the largest node-pair block, which on
higher-order elements exceeds the smallest by orders of magnitude.  Each pair therefore carries an
$8$-bit factor $r_{pq}=2^{-E}(1+m/8)\le1$, with a $5$-bit exponent $E$ and a $3$-bit mantissa $m$,
relative to the element scale $s_e$: $K_{pq}\approx s_e r_{pq}C_{pq}$ with $C_{pq}$ its nine
\texttt{int8} codes (Figure~\ref{fig:fmtelement}), as in microscaling formats~\cite{Rouhani2023} with
the node pair as the block.

\subsection{Assembled matrices}

BSR stores each node coupling once, as a $3\times3$ block with a column index
(Figure~\ref{fig:fmtbsr}), where the element formats store it once per element sharing it.  We
quantize its blocks after the generated single-precision assembly, in \texttt{int8} with a
single-precision scale per block or in \texttt{fp16} with one power-of-two scale.  The rows of the
assembled tangent also annihilate translations, so the difference form holds with $p,q$ ranging over
the nodes and the diagonal blocks are neither stored nor indexed.  Each pair of nodes $i<j$ is stored once: in the product $y=Kx$, with $x_i$ and $y_i$ the
three entries of node $i$ and $d=x_j-x_i$, its block adds $K_{ij}d$ to $y_i$ and $-K_{ij}^{\mathsf T}d$
to $y_j$, so the
off-diagonal blocks of the encoded matrix are symmetric by construction, which the assembled ones are
only to the rounding of its summation order; the implied diagonal blocks are symmetric only to the
encoding error.

Rows are held in the SELL-C-$\sigma$ format~\cite{Kreutzer2014}: $C=16$ rows side by side, sorted by
length within windows of $\sigma=32C$ rows and padded per group, so that one block position of $16$
rows is decoded as one vector; columns are $16$-bit offsets $j-i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:fmtqbsr}).  Each
thread owns whole windows and updates its own rows directly, lane after lane; blocks reaching beyond
its rows or the offsets go to a per-thread remainder whose updates of other threads' rows pass
through slots added after a barrier, so the product is deterministic and needs no atomics, where
symmetric products have otherwise been parallelised by colouring~\cite{Alappat2020,Elafrou2019}.  At
$10^7$ degrees of freedom on $72$ threads the remainder holds $\remainderShareLo$--$\remainderShareHi\%$
of the stored blocks.  The encoder writes the records ($13$ bytes per stored block in \texttt{int8}
against $36$ per assembled one) over the assembled values in rounds that never overwrite a value
not yet read.

\section{Encodings and the Newton iteration}

Every format is encoded once per Newton step, fused into its set-up, and decoded in registers
inside the application; no full-precision copy of a quadrature-point or element store exists.
\texttt{fp16} carries a power-of-two scale (per element, or per matrix for BSR).  \texttt{int8} maps
the largest magnitude of its block --- an element, a node pair or a matrix block --- to $127$.
Mean and deviation stores the element mean $\Sbar=\sum_q\Sq/|\widehat K|$ of a quadrature-point
store ($|\widehat K|$ the reference element's volume) in single precision and the deviations
$\Sq-w_q\Sbar$ in \texttt{int8} or \texttt{fp16}.

Let $\eps$ bound the encoding error $\Delta\mathcal{H}$ in the energy of the exact operator,
$|x^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathcal{H}x|\le\eps\,x^{\mathsf T}\mathcal{H}x$; for the matrix difference
forms, whose implied diagonal blocks are symmetric only to the encoding error, in the norm
$\|\mathcal{H}^{-1/2}\Delta\mathcal{H}\,\mathcal{H}^{-1/2}\|_2\le\eps$.  If the Krylov solves for the
Newton step $s$, $\mathcal{H}s=-f$ with $f$ the residual, stop at relative residual $\eta$, the step
satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\|f+\mathcal{H}s\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}}
  \le \eta\sqrt{\tfrac{1+\eps}{1-\eps}} + \tfrac{\eps(1+\eta)}{1-\eps} = \eta+\eps+O(\eps(\eta+\eps)),
  \label{eq:forcing}
\end{equation}
so the encoding adds to the forcing term~\cite{Catinas2001}, here in the energy norms of
affine-conjugate Newton methods~\cite{Deuflhard2004}: invisible for $\eps\ll\eta$, rate-determining for
$\eps\gg\eta$.  A codec rounds each number in proportion to the magnitude it scales by, and an
element tangent is close to singular on infinitesimal rotations at a stress-free state, so through
Korn's inequality what bounds $\eps$ is the \emph{absolute} rounding against the shear modulus.  Two
predictions follow for all formats: an encoding whose rounding is set by the largest
(bulk-dominated) entry of an element --- \texttt{int8} with one scale per element --- costs
iterations, one whose rounding follows each value or the variation over the element does not; and
an encoded matrix that does not annihilate translations has an error unbounded relative to the
operator on them, which the difference form removes.

\section{Implementation and setup}

\paragraph{Kernels}  The element formats traverse the mesh in blocks of $V$ elements, one per vector
lane, with the reference element's constants folded into generated code.  The application gathers
the increment, forms gradients or differences in the vector's precision, rounds them to single
precision, contracts with the decoded store ($81$ multiply--adds per point or moment), and scatters
through a packed two-pass layout~\cite{PackedMesh}: packs of elements with local node numbering accumulate without
atomics and shared nodes are reduced in a second pass.  Single-precision arithmetic adds a relative
error of $2^{-24}$, far below either encoding.  BSR is applied row-parallel, the quantized matrix as
in Section~3.3.  Every operator, BSR included, is applied as $y \leftarrow y + Ax$, its use inside
Newton.

\paragraph{On the GPU}  The whole Newton iteration runs on the device.  BSR is assembled by
our code's device kernels and applied with sixteen threads per block row; cuSPARSE's \texttt{bsrmv}
and its generic product on the matrix expanded to scalar CSR, its fastest path, are measured beside
it.  The compressed stores keep their layouts with blocks of $32$ elements, one
warp, and are set up on the device, giving the host's codes.  TET4, TET10 and HEX8 apply one thread
per element with atomic scatters or a block-local scatter in shared memory, whichever is faster;
HEX27 applies one thread per point (point stores) or one warp per element (element matrices), its
point store unfolded, since the threads of a warp would fold differently and save no work.  The
quantized matrix stores both blocks of a pair, since storing it once needs a transposed scatter, and
full $32$-bit columns, since $16$-bit offsets send the blocks out of their reach to a slower
remainder kernel: on the GPU both cost more than they save.

\paragraph{Machine and problems}  One Grace CPU of an NVIDIA GH200 node (CSCS Alps), $72$
Neoverse-V2 cores, LPDDR5X at a nominal $\MachineNominalBandwidth$~GB/s; GCC~13.3, \texttt{-O3 -mcpu=neoverse-v2}, $72$ OpenMP threads bound one
per core; vectors and residuals in double precision.  Neo-Hookean material on unit-cube meshes of
linear (TET4) and quadratic (TET10) tetrahedra, twelve per cell, and trilinear (HEX8) and
triquadratic (HEX27, $4^3$ Gauss points) hexahedra.  The application study uses $\benchdofHexEight$
degrees of freedom, a warped state and a white-noise increment.  The Newton study clamps one face
and displaces the opposite one at about $10^6$ and $10^7$ degrees of freedom, and twists the cube by
$90^\circ$ at $10^6$, in load steps that no format rejects (four to $32$ for the stretch, $16$ to
$256$ for the twist, by element and size); Newton to a relative residual of $10^{-8}$,
unpreconditioned conjugate gradients to $10^{-3}$ for every format.
The GPU is one H100 of the same node type, $96$~GB of HBM3 at a nominal $\gpuNominalBandwidth$~GB/s,
CUDA~12.6.

\paragraph{Use of AI}  An AI coding assistant (Claude Code, Anthropic) helped write and run the
benchmark and Newton drivers, the hand-optimised kernels, the job scripts, the log parsing behind the
tables and figures, and the format diagrams, under the author's direction.  All reported numbers are
generated from the run logs.

\section{Results on the H100 GPU}
\label{sec:gpu}

On the H100 our BSR product runs at the memory bandwidth
($\gpuBsrThirtyTwoGbsMin$--$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoGbsMax$~GB/s in single precision, of a nominal
$\gpuNominalBandwidth$~GB/s),
$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCusparseHexEight$--$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCusparseHexTwentySeven\times$ faster than
cuSPARSE's \texttt{bsrmv} and $\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMin$--$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMax\times$ faster than
its CSR product, which streams as fast but reads
$\gpuCsrThirtyTwoBytesVsBsrMin$--$\gpuCsrThirtyTwoBytesVsBsrMax\times$ the bytes
(Table~\ref{tab:gpu}).  The \texttt{int8} quantized matrix, which streams its records as BSR streams
its values, keeps its Grace gain and on three elements exceeds it, at $\gpuQbsrEightVsThirtyTwoTetFour$,
$\gpuQbsrEightVsThirtyTwoTetTen$, $\gpuQbsrEightVsThirtyTwoHexEight$ and
$\gpuQbsrEightVsThirtyTwoHexTwentySeven\times$ BSR's speed; the element formats, limited by gathers,
atomics and latency rather than bytes, gain less than on Grace --- on HEX27 the element matrices fall
to BSR's speed or below and the half-precision point store, at
$\gpuQpHalfVsThirtyTwoHexTwentySeven\times$, is the fastest operator.  The Newton iteration counts
are those of Grace, exactly for BSR and within a tenth for the \texttt{int8} formats, whose iteration
the order of the device's sums perturbs most; only the HEX27 twist spreads by a tenth on either
machine, BSR included.

The set-up weighs more, since the GPU speeds up the products more than the assembly: at about $10^7$
degrees of freedom the half-precision quantized matrix spends $\gpuTenmSetupShareQbsrHalfHexEight$,
$\gpuTenmSetupShareQbsrHalfTetTen$ and $\gpuTenmSetupShareQbsrHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ of its Newton run on
HEX8, TET10 and HEX27 in assembly and encoding ($\tenmSetupShareQbsrHalfHexEight$,
$\tenmSetupShareQbsrHalfTetTen$ and $\tenmSetupShareQbsrHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ on Grace), the point stores
about $\gpuTenmSetupShareQpHalfTetTen\%$.  The quantized matrix stays fastest on TET4
($\gpuTenmTimeQbsrHalfTetFour$ of single-precision BSR's time) and HEX8 ($\gpuTenmTimeQbsrEightHexEight$),
while on TET10 and HEX27 the quadrature-point stores lead ($\gpuTenmTimeQpEightMdTetTen$ in
\texttt{int8} mean and deviation and $\gpuTenmTimeQpHalfHexTwentySeven$ in \texttt{fp16};
Figure~\ref{fig:gputts}, Table~\ref{tab:summary}).

\section{Discussion and conclusions}

The choice of format follows the element.  Where a matrix row has few blocks and the element formats
repeat each coupling in every element sharing it --- linear and quadratic tetrahedra --- the quantized
assembled matrix is the fastest format.  Where rows are long and elements large --- triquadratic
hexahedra --- the element formats win, because they gather each node once per element where the
matrix gathers it once per block.  Trilinear hexahedra sit between.  Across all elements the element
formats keep the lowest peak memory; assembling and encoding the matrix in chunks of rows would
close part of that gap.  On the GPU, whose BSR product runs at the memory bandwidth, the bytes decide
less: the element formats keep the least of their saving, and the assembly that the quantized matrix
shares with BSR weighs more, so on the quadratic elements the quadrature-point stores lead.

Two properties decide which encodings are usable in a Newton iteration, for every format: the
rounding must follow the values or their variation over the element, not the largest entry of the
element, and the encoded operator must keep the rigid translations in its kernel.  Half precision and
\texttt{int8} mean-plus-deviation satisfy the first; the difference form provides the second and is
what makes \texttt{int8} usable for element and assembled matrices.

The gains need the operator to stream from memory: while the matrix fits in the last-level cache,
BSR is faster.  The study uses one material, structured meshes and an unpreconditioned Krylov
method; with a preconditioner built from the operator, the encodings change the preconditioner too.
