\begin{abstract}
Newton--Krylov solvers for finite-strain elasticity spend most of their time applying the tangent
operator, and how that operator is stored decides both the time and the memory of a solve.  The
assembled sparse matrix has the largest memory footprint, and its application is limited by memory
bandwidth; matrix-free application stores no tangent but is limited by arithmetic, since it
evaluates the material law at every quadrature point in every Krylov iteration.  Storing the tangent
in a compressed format reduces both the footprint and the bytes each application streams, lifting
the bandwidth limit of the assembled matrix without the arithmetic of matrix-free application.  The
encoding perturbs the tangent, however, and its effect on the Newton and Krylov iteration counts
must be assessed together with the speed-up it buys.

We develop three compressed formats of the tangent that span this range: the quantized block
sparse (BSR) matrix, the quantized element matrix, and the quadrature-point store, which keeps the
pulled-back material tangent where the quadrature evaluates it.  One set of measures reduces their
memory in two steps.  Exact reductions drop the numbers the application does not need: the symmetry
of the tangent leaves $45$ numbers per quadrature point and stores each pair of matrix blocks once;
the quadrature leaves only the values its rule can distinguish, moments against products of
gradient functions on quadratic tetrahedra and point values folded along the lines of quadrature
points on hexahedra, $\storeTetTen$ numbers per quadratic tetrahedron and $\storeHexEight$ per
trilinear hexahedron; and a difference form leaves out the diagonal node blocks of the matrix
formats, so that rigid translations stay exactly in the kernel of the encoded operator.  Encodings
then store what is left in fewer bits: scaled \texttt{fp16}, \texttt{int8}, and \texttt{int8} mean
plus deviation, with a scale per node pair for the element matrix, decoded inside the application.
We show that an
encoding error enters inexact Newton as an additive forcing term, set by the absolute rounding on
smooth, nearly rigid modes, which makes the choice of encoding a quantitative criterion, and we
model the bytes and operations per degree of freedom of each format against BSR.

We compare the formats with BSR in double and single precision on linear and quadratic tetrahedra
and trilinear and triquadratic hexahedra at $10^7$ degrees of freedom, on the 72-core Arm
Neoverse-V2 CPU and one H100 GPU of a GH200 node.  Half precision and \texttt{int8} mean plus
deviation keep the Newton iteration counts of the exact operator.  On the CPU the fastest format is
the quantized BSR matrix on linear and quadratic tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra, solving in
$\timePctQbsrHalfTetFour\%$, $\timePctQbsrHalfTetTen\%$ and $\timePctQbsrEightHexEight\%$ of the time of
single-precision BSR from $\QbsrHalfMemVsThirtyTwoTetTen$--$\QbsrEightMemVsThirtyTwoHexEight\times$
less operator memory, and the half-precision element matrix on triquadratic hexahedra,
solving in $\timePctEaDiffHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ of that time; the element formats keep the lowest peak memory, since they need
no assembled matrix.  On the GPU, against our tuned BSR product
$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMin$--$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoVsCsrMax\times$ faster than cuSPARSE's fastest for our problems of interest, the
quantized BSR matrix solves in $\gpuTenmTimePctQbsrHalfTetFour\%$ and $\gpuTenmTimePctQbsrEightHexEight\%$ of
the time of single-precision BSR on linear tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra, and the quadrature-point stores in
$\gpuTenmTimePctQpEightMdTetTen\%$ and $\gpuTenmTimePctQpHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ on the quadratic elements, where
the assembly the matrix formats need weighs more than on the CPU.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Newton--Krylov solvers for finite-strain elasticity apply the tangent operator tens to hundreds of
times per Newton step, and the format in which that operator is stored decides both the time and
the memory of a solve.  The formats span a range.  An assembled sparse matrix, in block compressed
sparse row (BSR) form for a vector-valued problem, pays a large assembly once per step and then
applies at the speed of the memory system.  Our BSR product in double precision sustains
$\MachineBandwidth$~GB/s on the Grace CPU used here, $\MachineBandwidthPct\%$ of its nominal
$\MachineNominalBandwidth$~GB/s, and $\gpuBsrSixtyFourGbsMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourGbsMax$~GB/s on the
H100 GPU, $\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMax\%$ of its nominal
$\gpuNominalBandwidth$~GB/s, where it is $\gpuBsrSixtyFourVsCsrMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourVsCsrMax\times$
faster than cuSPARSE's fastest product on the same matrix; neither kernel has headroom left.
Element matrices store each element's dense block and apply it with a gather and a scatter.  A
matrix-free application re-evaluates the constitutive law at every quadrature point of every element
on every application, which is compute-bound and repeats identical work, since the state is frozen
within the step.  Moving along this range trades stored bytes against arithmetic, and quantizing the
stored numbers shifts the trade for every format at once.

Partial assembly sits between the element matrix and the matrix-free application: it stores a
per-element object that captures geometry, state and material, and applies it by a small tensor
contraction.  Stored at every quadrature point, the pulled-back material tangent makes the
application exact, at $45$ numbers per point, and the quadrature sees fewer than that.  Memory can
therefore be saved in two ways, for every format: exactly, by not storing what the application does
not need, and approximately, by storing the rest in fewer bits.  Only the second perturbs the
tangent.

That perturbation matters for the nonlinear iteration.  Section~\ref{sec:newton} shows that a
relative perturbation $\eps$ of the tangent enters an inexact-Newton iteration as an additive
forcing term: an iteration whose linear solves stop at a relative residual $\eta$ converges, to
first order, as one whose forcing term is $\eta+\eps$.  With the Krylov tolerances used inside
Newton ($\eta = 10^{-2}$ to $10^{-3}$), an encoding error of $10^{-2}$ is not small, and the
iteration loses steps.

This paper asks which format, with which encoding, gives the shortest time to solution and the
smallest memory for a Newton--Krylov hyperelasticity solve, and why.  The answer depends on three
things the formats share: the bytes each streams per degree of freedom, the arithmetic and gathering
each performs, and the perturbation each encoding makes to the tangent, which the iteration either
absorbs or pays for in iterations.  The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Exact reductions of the stored tangent} (Sections~\ref{sec:formulation},
  \ref{sec:elementmatrix} and~\ref{sec:qbsr}), which hold whatever the encoding.  The major symmetry
  of the tangent leaves $45$ numbers per quadrature point, the upper triangle of an element matrix
  and one block per pair of nodes of the assembled matrix.  The quadrature leaves fewer: per
  component the application sees only as many values as the rank of the products of reference
  derivatives at the points, which we derive for each element and attain, with moments on quadratic
  tetrahedra and, on hexahedra, a fold of the point values along the lines of quadrature points that
  also removes multiply--adds.  The difference form leaves out the diagonal node blocks of the
  element and assembled matrices, implied by their row sums, and keeps rigid translations exactly in
  the kernel of the encoded operator.  On trilinear hexahedra the reductions remove
  $\exactQpPctHexEight\%$ of the numbers of the point store, $\exactEaPctHexEight\%$ of the element
  matrix and $\exactBsrPctHexEight\%$ of the assembled matrix (Table~\ref{tab:measures}).
\item \emph{Encodings, an error model and the Newton consequence} (Sections~\ref{sec:compression}
  and~\ref{sec:newton}), common to the formats: scaled \texttt{fp16} and \texttt{int8} and
  mean-plus-deviation encodings, and a scale per node pair for element matrices, decoded inside the
  application.  The error that matters is the absolute rounding on the smooth, nearly rigid modes,
  bounded through Korn's inequality, and it enters inexact Newton as an additive forcing term, which
  turns the choice of codec into a quantitative criterion.
\item \emph{Three formats built from these measures} (Sections~\ref{sec:elementmatrix}
  to~\ref{sec:gpukernels}): the quadrature-point store with its set-up and application kernels, element
  matrices built from the same stored tensors, and the quantized assembled matrix in a
  SELL-C-$\sigma$ layout applied without atomics, each on the CPU and the GPU.
\item \emph{A cost model against BSR} (Section~\ref{sec:model}): operator memory after each
  measure, compulsory traffic and operation counts per degree of freedom for each element and
  encoding, and the arithmetic rate an element format must sustain to match BSR.
\item \emph{An evaluation on one Grace CPU and one H100 GPU} (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:appmesh}) for four
  element types up to $10^7$ degrees of freedom: throughput, operator and peak memory, set-up, Newton
  iteration counts and time to solution of every format against BSR in double and single precision,
  across problem sizes, and the resulting choice of format per element and machine
  (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Partial assembly and quadrature-point data.}  Storing geometric factors at quadrature
points and applying an operator by tensor contraction originates in spectral-element methods
\cite{Orszag1980,DevilleFischerMund2002} and underlies the matrix-free frameworks of deal.II
\cite{KronbichlerKormann2012}, MFEM \cite{MFEM2021} and libCEED \cite{libCEED2021}.  For
finite-strain hyperelasticity, which quantities to keep at the quadrature points has been studied
explicitly, and the answer has been a choice along a single axis between memory and arithmetic.
Davydov et al.~\cite{Davydov2020} compare three caching levels for compressible neo-Hookean
elasticity --- a scalar, the Kirchhoff stress with two scalars, and the stress with the full
symmetric fourth-order spatial tangent --- and find the last nearly as fast as the second while
being independent of the constitutive law, and faster than a sparse matrix for polynomial degree
above one in three dimensions.  Brown et al.~\cite{Brown2022} store between $17$ and $31$ scalars
per point (deformation and stress quantities, in the current or the initial configuration) and
model the assembled alternative at about one flop per six bytes.  Schussnig et
al.~\cite{Schussnig2025} add storage levels for anisotropic laws, and Wichrowski et
al.~\cite{Wichrowski2025} store the spatial tangent and stress of automatically differentiated
materials, $27$ numbers per point besides the geometry, at no cost over hand-written kernels.
Kronbichler and Kormann~\cite{KronbichlerKormann2019} make the same trade for the geometry alone,
from mapping support points to merged coefficients.  Every one of these stores its data in double
precision; we found no prior work that quantises quadrature-point data, or that stores what the
quadrature can see of it rather than its point values.

\paragraph{Reduced-precision storage.}  Decoupling the storage precision of operator data from the
arithmetic precision is established in sparse linear algebra: block-Jacobi preconditioners whose
blocks are stored in half, single or double precision chosen per block \cite{Anzt2019}, and memory
accessors that hide the storage format of a sparse product behind its loads
\cite{Gruetzmacher2023}; see \cite{HighamMary2022,Abdelfattah2021} for surveys.  For sparse
matrix--vector products the precision has been chosen per non-zero \cite{Ahmad2019} or per row
\cite{Tezcan2022} by the magnitude of the entries, among floating-point formats; adaptive-precision
products go down to an eight-bit format, but only for the smallest entries, the bulk staying in $16$
to $64$ bits \cite{Graillat2024}.  Finite-element solvers have run their matrix--vector products in a
mix of $16$-, $21$-, $32$- and $64$-bit formats \cite{Ichimura2018} and on INT8 tensor cores, with a
constant voxel element matrix held exactly in eight-bit integers and the vectors split into
eight-bit digits to double-precision accuracy \cite{Ichimura2024}, and the rounding error of cell
kernels and assembly in mixed single and half precision has been analysed
\cite{CrociWells2024}.  We found no comparison of reduced-precision formats across the assembled,
element and quadrature levels, and no stiffness matrix with every entry encoded in eight bits.
Storing the matrices of a multigrid hierarchy in low precision can make them indefinite
\cite{Tamstorf2021}; writing each row as a sum of differences, as edge-based schemes do
\cite{Lohner2008}, keeps constants in the kernel, but we found no encoding that uses it to keep the
rigid translations in the kernel of the encoded operator.  Matrix-free
multigrid runs its V-cycle in single precision inside a double-precision outer iteration
\cite{KronbichlerLjungkvist2019}.  Fixed-rate block compression with a shared exponent per block
and a decorrelating transform \cite{Lindstrom2014} motivates two of our design choices: one scale
per block of values and a transform (here the subtraction of the element mean) that makes what is
quantised small.

\paragraph{Newton with an approximate Jacobian.}  Inexact Newton methods
\cite{DemboEisenstatSteihaug1982} converge locally linearly when the linear residual is reduced by
a forcing term $\eta<1$, and superlinearly when $\eta\to0$; forcing-term strategies are analysed by
Eisenstat and Walker \cite{EisenstatWalker1996}.  With a Jacobian error $\Delta$ and an exact
residual, the error contracts as $O(\|e\|^2 + (\eta+\Delta)\|e\|)$ \cite{Kelley2018}: linear
convergence at a rate proportional to the forcing term plus the Jacobian error, with no floor on
the attainable accuracy.  C\u{a}tina\c{s} \cite{Catinas2001} treats such perturbed Jacobians, solved
inexactly, as inexact Newton steps with a modified forcing term, and Kelley \cite{Kelley2022}
analyses Jacobians stored and factored in reduced precision.  For convex problems, affine-conjugate
Newton methods measure residuals and steps in the energy norm of the tangent
\cite{Deuflhard2004}, the norm conjugate gradients controls.  Section~\ref{sec:newton} gives the specific form
this takes when the perturbation is an element-wise relative error of a symmetric positive
definite tangent.

\section{Operator formats}
\label{sec:overview}

All formats of this paper apply the same operator, the tangent of the hyperelastic residual at the
current Newton iterate; they differ in how much of it the set-up computes once per Newton step and
how much every application recomputes (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).  Matrix-free application stores
nothing and evaluates the material law and its derivative at every quadrature point of every element
in every Krylov iteration.  The quadrature-point store (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}) keeps the
pulled-back tangent at the points, so that an application forms the gradients of the increment,
decodes and contracts.  The element matrix (Section~\ref{sec:elementmatrix}) contracts the stored
tangent with the basis once per step, so that an application is one dense product per element.  The
assembled matrix (Section~\ref{sec:qbsr}) sums the element matrices into the pattern of node
couplings, each coupling once, so that an application is one block product per matrix row.

The memory of the three stored formats is reduced in two steps (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b).  Exact
reductions remove numbers the application does not need and leave the operator unchanged: the major
symmetry of the tangent; the quadrature, which sees only some functionals of the point values
(Section~\ref{sec:minimal}); and the difference form, which leaves out the diagonal node blocks of the
matrices (Section~\ref{sec:elementmatrix}).  Encodings then store what remains in fewer bits
(Section~\ref{sec:compression}); only they perturb the operator, and Section~\ref{sec:newton} relates
the perturbation to the Krylov tolerance.  Sections~\ref{sec:kernels} and~\ref{sec:gpukernels} give
the kernels on the CPU and the GPU, Section~\ref{sec:model} a cost model against BSR, and
Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:appmesh} the measurements.

\section{Quadrature-point stores}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{The tangent action at the quadrature points}

Let $K$ be an element with reference cell $\refcell$, reference basis
$\{\varphi_p\}_{p=1}^{N}$, Jacobian $J$, and quadrature points $\xhat_q$ with weights $w_q$,
$q=1,\dots,n_q$.  For a hyperelastic material with first Piola--Kirchhoff stress $P(F)$ and tangent
$A_{ijkl}=\partial P_{ij}/\partial F_{kl}$ \cite{BonetWood2008}, the pulled-back tangent at a point
is
\begin{equation}
  \Sq_{ikmn} \;=\; w_q \sum_{j,l} A_{ijkl}\bigl(F(\xhat_q)\bigr)\,
      J^{-1}_{nj}(\xhat_q)\, J^{-1}_{ml}(\xhat_q)\, \det J(\xhat_q) ,
  \label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
with the quadrature weight folded in, and the element tangent action on an increment $h$ is
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{ip}
  \;=\; \sum_{q=1}^{n_q} \sum_{k,m,n} \Sq_{ikmn}\;
        \partial_m h_k(\xhat_q)\; \partial_n \varphi_p(\xhat_q) ,
  \qquad \partial_m = \partial/\partial\xi_m .
  \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:action} is the exact matrix-free action, quadrature included.  Everything in it
except $\Sq$ belongs to the reference element.  For a material derived from a strain energy the
tangent has the major symmetry $\Sq_{ikmn}=\Sq_{kinm}$, under exchange of (output component, test
direction) with (increment component, increment direction), so each point carries $45$ independent
numbers in three dimensions; a material defined by its residual has no major symmetry and carries
$81$.  Storing
$\Sq$ for every point makes the application exact and independent of geometry, state and
material, at $45\,n_q$ numbers per element.

Fewer numbers per point are possible in the spatial frame, where the tangent splits into a
fourth-order part with minor symmetries and a stress term, $21+6$ numbers, as in the prior work
above; the application must then push each point's gradient forward through the current
configuration's Jacobian, nine more numbers and two $3\times3$ products per point.  That trades
arithmetic for bytes, and Section~\ref{sec:model} shows the application to be limited by its
arithmetic on TET10 and HEX8, so we keep the pulled-back form, whose application touches no
geometry.

\subsection{What the quadrature can see}
\label{sec:minimal}

The tangent enters \eqref{eq:action} only multiplied by products of reference derivatives of the
basis.  Let $V_m=\mathrm{span}\{\partial_m\varphi_j\}_j$ be the space of reference derivatives in
direction $m$, $r=\dim(V_1+V_2+V_3)$ the dimension of all of them together, and
$\Pi_{mn}=\mathrm{span}\{u\,v : u\in V_m,\ v\in V_n\}$ the product space of a direction pair.  In
\eqref{eq:action} the component $\Sq_{ikmn}$ is multiplied by $\partial_m h_k(\xhat_q)\,
\partial_n\varphi_p(\xhat_q)$, the value at $\xhat_q$ of a function in $\Pi_{mn}$, so it enters the
action only through the functionals
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\chi\bigl(S_{ikmn}\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{q} \chi(\xhat_q)\, \Sq_{ikmn} ,
  \qquad \chi \in \Pi_{mn} .
  \label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
Write $s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_q}$ for the point values of one component and $E_{mn}$ for the evaluation
matrix whose rows are the values $(\chi(\xhat_q))_q$ of the products
$\chi=\partial_m\varphi_a\,\partial_n\varphi_b$ that span $\Pi_{mn}$.  The functionals
\eqref{eq:moments} are the entries of $E_{mn}s$, so the action depends on $s$ only through its
projection onto the row space of $E_{mn}$: any $\rho_{mn}=\operatorname{rank}E_{mn}$ functionals whose
rows span that space reproduce \eqref{eq:action} exactly, and no fewer do.  Since $E_{mn}$ has $n_q$
columns and its row space is the image of $\Pi_{mn}$, $\rho_{mn}\le\min(n_q,\dim\Pi_{mn})$.  The
minimal exact store is $\sum\rho_{mn}$ over the $45$ independent components; Table~\ref{tab:stores}
lists $r$, $\dim\Pi_{mn}$ and $\rho_{mn}$, computed by the cost model of Section~\ref{sec:model} from
the reference basis and the rule.

The counts follow from the shape of the derivatives.  On TET4 the derivatives are constant
($r=1$) and the single point is the whole store.  On TET10 the derivatives of the quadratic basis
are the linear functions ($r=4$) in every direction, and their products span $P_2$,
$\dim\Pi_{mn}=10$.  The $11$-point rule integrates degree $4$ exactly, so a $\chi\in P_2$ that
vanishes at every point has $\int\chi^2=\sum_q w_q\chi(\xhat_q)^2=0$ and is zero, whatever the sign
of the weights: $E_{mn}$ is injective on $P_2$, $\rho_{mn}=10$, and ten moments replace eleven point
values.  On HEX8 the derivative in direction $m$ is constant in $\xi_m$: $V_1=\mathrm{span}\{1,\xi_2,
\xi_3,\xi_2\xi_3\}$, and the three directions together span every trilinear monomial but
$\xi_1\xi_2\xi_3$ ($r=7$).  For $m\ne n$ the products span $12$ functions, which the $2^3$ Gauss
points distinguish only as far as the trilinear ones, $\rho_{mn}=8$.  For $m=n$ the products do not
depend on $\xi_m$ ($\dim\Pi_{mm}=9$), and the Gauss points come in pairs that differ only in
$\xi_m$, so the action sees only the sum of each pair: $\rho_{mm}=4$.  Of the $45$ components $18$
have $m=n$, and the minimum is $18\cdot4+27\cdot8=\minimalHexEight$ against $\storeHexEight$ point
values.  On HEX27 the derivative in direction $m$ is linear in $\xi_m$ ($r=26$, every triquadratic
monomial but $\xi_1^2\xi_2^2\xi_3^2$); for $m=n$ the products are quadratic in $\xi_m$, and the four
Gauss points of a line see three moments along it, $\rho_{mm}=3\cdot4^2=48$, while for $m\ne n$
$\rho_{mn}=64=n_q$, a minimum of $\minimalHexTwentySeven$ against $\storeHexTwentySeven$.

The kernels of this paper hold the minimum on every element: the moments on TET10, which have a
schedule of their own (Section~\ref{sec:moments}), and on HEX8 and HEX27 the point values folded
along the lines of points (Section~\ref{sec:fold}).

\subsection{Moments on quadratic tetrahedra}
\label{sec:moments}

On TET10, write $\lambda_a$ for the barycentric coordinates.  The reference
derivatives are linear, $\partial_m h_k = \sum_{a} \lambda_a P_{kma}$ with $P_{kma}$ the derivative
at vertex $a$, and similarly $\partial_n\varphi_p = \sum_b \lambda_b G_{pnb}$ with rational
constants $G$.  Substituting into \eqref{eq:action} gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{ip} \;=\; \sum_{n,b} G_{pnb} \sum_{a}\sum_{k,m} \Mab_{ikmn}\, P_{kma} ,\\
  \Mab \;=\; \sum_q \lambda_a(\xhat_q)\lambda_b(\xhat_q)\, \Sq ,
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:tet10moments}
\end{equation}
with $\Mab=M^{ba}$: ten tensors of $45$ numbers, the $P_2$ moments of the tangent in the
Bernstein basis.  Each $\Mab$ is a positively weighted local average of the tangent when the rule's
weights are positive, which matters for the encodings of Section~\ref{sec:compression}; the
$11$-point rule has one negative weight, so this holds approximately.

The moments change the schedule as well as the store (Figure~\ref{fig:moments}).  The application
compresses the increment into its derivatives at the four vertices, $P_{akm}$, with rational
constants; each stored moment is decoded once and applied to both $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$, sixteen
$9\times9$ block contractions in all; and the result is expanded to the ten nodes with the transposed
constants (Algorithm~\ref{alg:applymoments}).  No gradient is formed at a quadrature point.

\subsection{The element mean and the deviations}
\label{sec:mean}

Two encodings of Section~\ref{sec:compression} split the store into an element mean and deviations
from it.  The element mean is $\Sbar = \sum_q \Sq/|\refcell|$, with $|\refcell|$ the volume of the
reference cell; since $\Sq$ carries the weight $w_q$, $\Sbar$ is the weighted mean of the pointwise
tangent.  At the points the deviations are $\Sq-w_q\Sbar$.  For the moments of
\eqref{eq:tet10moments} the mean contributes $X_{ab}\Sbar$, with
$X_{ab}=\sum_q w_q\lambda_a(\xhat_q)\lambda_b(\xhat_q)$ the Gram matrix of the barycentric
coordinates, and the deviations are $D^{ab}=\Mab-X_{ab}\Sbar$.  Both sets sum to zero over the
element.  Where the tangent varies little over the element the deviations are small relative to
$\Sbar$, which is what makes them cheap to encode (Section~\ref{sec:meandev}).

\subsection{The fold on the hexahedra}
\label{sec:fold}

On a tensor-product element the minimum of Table~\ref{tab:stores} has an explicit form that keeps
the point values and drops some of them.  Let the element carry Lagrange polynomials of degree $p$
in each reference direction, integrated with the $q_1^3$-point Gauss rule, whose points in one
direction are $x_0<\dots<x_{q_1-1}$: HEX8 has $p=1$, $q_1=2$, HEX27 has $p=2$, $q_1=4$.  Fix a direction
$d$ and a component $\Sq_{ikmn}$ with $m=n=d$.  In \eqref{eq:action} it multiplies the value at
$\xhat_q$ of $\chi=\partial_d h_k\,\partial_d\varphi_p$.  A derivative in $\xi_d$ lowers the degree in
$\xi_d$ by one, so on a line of points in direction $d$ --- the $q_1$ points that share their other
two coordinates --- $\chi$ is a polynomial of degree $2(p-1)$ in $\xi_d$: constant on HEX8,
quadratic on HEX27.  Both satisfy $2(p-1)\le q_1-2$, so the values of $\chi$ at any $q_1-1$ of the
line's points determine it.  With $D$ the index of the point left out and $\ell_j$ the Lagrange
polynomials of the others, $\chi(x_D)=\sum_{j\ne D}\ell_j(x_D)\,\chi(x_j)$, and the line's share of
the action is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \sum_{j} \widehat S^{q_j}_{ikdd}\,\chi(x_j) \;=\; \sum_{j\ne D} T^{q_j}_{ikdd}\,\chi(x_j),\\
  T^{q_j}_{ikdd} \;=\; \widehat S^{q_j}_{ikdd} + \ell_j(x_D)\,\widehat S^{q_D}_{ikdd} ,
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:fold}
\end{equation}
where $q_j$ is the point of the line with index $j$ in direction $d$.  The identity holds for every
increment and every test function, so storing $T$ at the line's other points and nothing at $q_D$
leaves the application exact, whatever the tangent, the geometry or the state.  We call this the
fold.  Each line keeps $q_1-1$ values of each such component, and there are $q_1^2$ lines in each
direction: $(q_1-1)q_1^2$ values, the rank $\rho_{dd}$ of Table~\ref{tab:stores} ($4$ on HEX8, $48$ on
HEX27).  The fold is therefore one choice of the minimal functionals of \eqref{eq:moments}: point
values of a modified tangent, so the stages that form the gradients at the points and return the
outputs from them are unchanged.

The other components do not fold.  For $m\ne n$ the product $\partial_m h_k\,\partial_n\varphi_p$ has
degree $(p-1)+p=2p-1=q_1-1$ in $\xi_m$ and in $\xi_n$, and $2p$ in the third direction, so every
point of every line is needed: $\rho_{mn}=n_q$, as Table~\ref{tab:stores} lists.  The folded
components are those whose test and increment directions agree: on the pairing of
Section~\ref{sec:formulation} they form, for each $d$, the $3\times3$ block over $(i,k)$ of the $9\times9$
tangent with $n=m=d$, symmetric, six packed numbers.  We drop the point with the same index $D$ on
every line, so a point drops direction $d$ exactly when its index in direction $d$ is $D$.  Writing
$d_q$ for the number of directions point $q$ drops, it stores $45-6d_q$ numbers and its
contraction loses the nine entries of each dropped block, $81-9d_q$ multiply--adds instead of $81$.
Over the element $3q_1^2$ point--direction pairs drop: the store holds $45n_q-18q_1^2$ numbers,
$\minimalHexEight$ on HEX8 and $\minimalHexTwentySeven$ on HEX27 instead of $\perpointHexEight$ and
$\perpointHexTwentySeven$, and the contraction saves $27q_1^2$ of its $81n_q$ multiply--adds, $108$ of
$648$ and $432$ of $5184$.  Figure~\ref{fig:fmtfold} shows HEX8: a point keeps $45$, $39$, $33$ or $27$
numbers.

\paragraph{Which point to drop.}  The weights $\ell_j(x_D)$ enter the stored values, and the
encodings scale by their largest magnitude.  On HEX8 the line has two points and the weight is $1$:
the kept point stores the sum of the two.  On HEX27 dropping an end point extrapolates, with
weights $1$, $-2.53$, $2.53$ on the other three points; dropping an interior point interpolates, with
weights $0.39$, $1$, $-0.39$.  We drop the second point of each line, so every folded value is at
most twice the largest point value, $|T|\le(1+|\ell_j|)\max_q|\Sq|\le 2\max_q|\Sq|$, on either
element.

\section{Encodings and their error}
\label{sec:compression}

\subsection{Encodings}

The stored numbers of an element are encoded with one of the codecs below, decoded in registers
inside the application, and never expanded to a full-precision copy in memory.  Encoding is fused
into the update kernel: a block of elements computes its point tangents (or moments), reduces the
magnitudes the codec needs, and writes the encoded values.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
\item[\texttt{fp16}, scaled.]  Half precision with one power-of-two scale per element, so that the
  largest magnitude sits just below the top of the half-precision range and the rescaling is
  exact.  Relative rounding $\ucodec = 2^{-11}$ of each value.
\item[\texttt{int8}, per element.]  Symmetric integers in $[-127,127]$ with one scale per element,
  $s=\max|S|/127$.  Because the action is linear in the store, the scale multiplies the element's
  outputs rather than the stored values, and decoding is a conversion.  Absolute rounding $s/2$.
\item[\texttt{int8}, per component.]  One scale per component, shared by the element's points or
  moments.  Components of the tangent differ in magnitude far more than one component varies over
  an element, so this captures the dynamic range where it is.  Decoding is a conversion and one
  multiply.
\item[\texttt{int8}, mean and deviation.]  The element mean $\Sbar$ in single precision, plus the
  deviations of Section~\ref{sec:mean} as \texttt{int8} with one scale per element.  Decoding is a conversion and one fused multiply--add,
  $\Sq\approx w_q\Sbar + s\,c^q$ at a point, $\Mab\approx X_{ab}\Sbar + s\,c^{ab}$ for a moment.
\item[\texttt{fp16}, mean and deviation.]  The same split with the deviations in half precision
  under a power-of-two scale per element: two bytes per number instead of one, and the rounding of
  half precision relative to each deviation rather than to the largest.
\end{description}

Figure~\ref{fig:codecs} draws what each encoding keeps of one component over an element's points.
Half precision rounds every number relative to itself; \texttt{int8} with one scale per element rounds
every number to a step set by the element's largest; the mean-and-deviation encodings code only the
variation of the tangent over the element.

The codecs are those already used for compressed history variables in viscoelastic constitutive
updates in our code, so the encoders, scale rules and device implementations exist; what
is new is decoding inside the application rather than in a separate pass.

\paragraph{The fold and the encodings.}  The fold of Section~\ref{sec:fold} is linear, so it commutes with the split of
Section~\ref{sec:mean}: it is applied to the deviations, and a folded component of point $q_j$
carries the reference weight $X_{j\alpha}=X_{q_j}+\ell_j(x_D)\,X_{q_D}$ in place of $X_{q_j}$, which is
the weight \eqref{eq:decode} decodes with.  The element mean and its $45$ numbers are unchanged.
Since the folded values reach up to twice the point values, the element scale can double, and the
encoding error with it.  The half-precision and mean-and-deviation stores keep their Newton and
Krylov counts (Section~\ref{sec:newtonresults}); \texttt{int8} with one scale per element, already
unusable as a Newton operator, needs a few more iterations on HEX27.

\subsection{Error model}
\label{sec:errormodel}

What matters for the iteration (Section~\ref{sec:newton}) is the error in the energy of the
operator, relative to the operator.  Let $\mathcal{H}_K$ be the exact element operator, assumed
positive semi-definite, and $\Delta\mathcal{H}_K$ the error the encoding introduces into it.  Both
vanish on increments with zero gradient, and we define
\begin{equation}
  \eps \;=\; \max_K\ \sup_{x}\ \frac{\bigl|x^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathcal{H}_K\,x\bigr|}
                                      {x^{\mathsf T}\mathcal{H}_K\,x} ,
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \bigl|x^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathcal{H}\,x\bigr| \;\le\; \eps\; x^{\mathsf T}\mathcal{H}\,x
  \label{eq:eps}
\end{equation}
for the assembled operators, since summing element-wise relative bounds of semi-definite terms
preserves them.  A codec rounds each stored number with an error proportional to the magnitude it
scales by.  If the point tangents are positive definite with smallest eigenvalue $\mu$ on the
nine-dimensional space of gradient directions, $\eps$ is the codec's rounding times a conditioning
factor:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \eps \;\lesssim\; c\,\ucodec\;\frac{\sigma}{\mu_q} ,\\
  \sigma \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \max|\Sq| & \text{\texttt{fp16}, \texttt{int8} per element}\\
              & \text{\quad or per component,}\\
    \max|\Sq - w_q\Sbar| & \text{mean and deviation,}
  \end{cases}
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:codecerror}
\end{equation}
with $\ucodec=2^{-11}$ for \texttt{fp16}, $\ucodec=2^{-8}$ for \texttt{int8}, and $c$ a modest
constant from the norm equivalence on $\R^{9\times9}$ and the quadrature.  This is an estimate, not
a bound: the $11$-point tetrahedral rule has a negative weight, so the element operator's
definiteness is not inherited point by point, and the measured errors are what count.  The ratio
$\max|\Sq|/\mu_q$ is the pointwise
conditioning of the tangent.  For an isotropic material near the reference configuration it is
$(\lambda+2\mu)/\mu = 2(1-\nu)/(1-2\nu)$: $3.5$ at Poisson ratio $\nu=0.3$, $11$ at $0.45$, $51$
at $0.49$.  An encoding that rounds the bulk-dominated entries at their own magnitude perturbs the
shear modes by that much, and the plain codecs lose a factor of the conditioning.  The
mean-and-deviation encoding reduces $\sigma$ to the variation of the tangent over the element,
which for TET10 at a moderate distortion is about $10^{-2}$ of its magnitude, but
keeps the conditioning factor.

\paragraph{Rotations.}  The element-wise bound \eqref{eq:eps} is finite only when the encoding
error vanishes wherever the element operator does.  It does on translations, whose gradient is
zero, but not on infinitesimal rotations: at a stress-free state the element tangent is singular on
them (it is positive only through the stress, in the geometric term), while an encoding error in
the stored numbers is not.  The element-wise ratio then has no bound, and the global one rests
instead on Korn's inequality: summing $|x^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathcal{H}_K x| \le \|\Delta S\|\,
\|\nabla x\|^2_K$ over elements and bounding $\|\nabla x\|^2$ by $C_{\mathrm{K}}\,x^{\mathsf T}
\mathcal{H}x/\mu$ gives $\eps \lesssim C_{\mathrm{K}}\,\|\Delta S\|/\mu$, with $C_{\mathrm{K}}$ the
Korn constant of the domain and its boundary conditions.  Two consequences: the error that matters
is the \emph{absolute} rounding of the stored numbers, measured against the shear modulus, not
the rounding relative to each number; and the modes that see it most are the smooth, locally
near-rigid ones the Newton iteration is made of, which a white-noise increment under-represents.
Encodings whose absolute error is set by the variation of the tangent over the element (mean and
deviation) are favoured.

Two consequences shape the measurements.  The plain \texttt{fp16} and \texttt{int8} encodings are
predicted to be adequate for compressible materials and to degrade with the bulk-to-shear ratio,
so the study sweeps the Poisson ratio.  And the predicted errors are to be compared not with
machine precision but with the Krylov forcing term, which is the subject of the next section.

\paragraph{Mean and deviation in the application.}\label{sec:meandev}  The mean-and-deviation encodings change the decoding, not the schedule: each stored number is
decoded as $w_q\Sbar + s\,c^q$ (or $X_{ab}\Sbar + s\,c^{ab}$) in registers, with $c$ the code and $s$
the element's scale, and the schedule of Section~\ref{sec:schedules} runs unchanged on the decoded
values.

\section{Effect on the Newton iteration}
\label{sec:newton}

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be the exact tangent at the current iterate, symmetric positive definite, and
$\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}+\Delta\mathcal{H}$ the encoded one, with \eqref{eq:eps}.
The quadrature-point stores encode a symmetric $\Sq$ and give a symmetric $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}$.
The element and assembled matrices in difference form do not quite: their off-diagonal blocks are
symmetric, but the implied diagonal blocks sum independently rounded blocks and carry an
antisymmetric part of the size of the encoding error (Section~\ref{sec:qbsr}), which
\eqref{eq:eps}, a bound on $x^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathcal{H}x$, does not see.  For them we read $\eps$
as the bound $\|\mathcal{H}^{-1/2}\Delta\mathcal{H}\,\mathcal{H}^{-1/2}\|_2\le\eps$, which reduces
to \eqref{eq:eps} for a symmetric error, the two-sided bounds below as bounds on the symmetric part
of $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}$, and conjugate gradients runs on an operator symmetric only to that
error; the Newton study measures the two together.
Krylov iterations on $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}s=-f$ stop at a step with relative residual $\eta$.
measured in the norm $\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}$ that conjugate gradients
controls.  By \eqref{eq:eps}, $(1-\eps)\mathcal{H}\preceq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\preceq(1+\eps)
\mathcal{H}$, so the two energy norms are equivalent; writing $f+\mathcal{H}s = (f+\widetilde
{\mathcal{H}}s) - \Delta\mathcal{H}s$ and bounding each term in the norm of the exact tangent gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\|f + \mathcal{H}s\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}}
  \;\le\; \eta\,\sqrt{\frac{1+\eps}{1-\eps}} \;+\; \frac{\eps\,(1+\eta)}{1-\eps}
  \;=\; \eta + \eps + O\bigl(\eps(\eta+\eps)\bigr) .
  \label{eq:forcing}
\end{equation}
The encoded tangent therefore turns an inexact Newton
iteration with forcing term $\eta$ into one with forcing term $\eta+\eps$ to first order --- the
reduction of \cite{Catinas2001}, here in the energy norm of \cite{Deuflhard2004} --- and the
local theory of \cite{DemboEisenstatSteihaug1982} applies with that forcing term: linear
convergence at a rate of order $\eta+\eps$, measured in the norm natural to the problem.  The bound
does not involve the condition number of $\mathcal{H}$, because $\eps$ is a relative bound in its
own energy.

Three cases follow, and they are the predictions the Newton study tests.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
\item $\eps\ll\eta$: the encoding is invisible to the iteration.  Newton and Krylov iteration
  counts equal those of the exact tangent.
\item $\eps\approx\eta$: the effective forcing term doubles; one more Newton step may be needed
  near the solution.
\item $\eps\gg\eta$: the iteration converges at rate $\eps$ regardless of the Krylov tolerance,
  and tightening the Krylov tolerance buys nothing.
\end{itemize}
The bound is first order and assumes a positive definite tangent; away from the solution, and for
energies that lose convexity, it is a guide rather than a guarantee, which is why the iteration
counts are measured rather than inferred.

\section{Element matrices}
\label{sec:elementmatrix}

The quadrature-point store is one end of a trade between what is stored and what is computed in
the application; the element matrix is the other.  Contracting the stored tensors with the basis
derivatives once per Newton step gives each element's symmetric $3N\times3N$ matrix,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  K_{(i,p),(k,q)} \;=\; {}& \sum_{j} \sum_{n,m}\, \partial_n\varphi_p(\xhat_{b_j})\,
      T^{j}_{\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)}\, \partial_m\varphi_q(\xhat_{a_j})\\
      &+\; (a_j\leftrightarrow b_j \text{ when } a_j\ne b_j) ,
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:elementmatrix}
\end{equation}
directly from the tensors of Section~\ref{sec:minimal} and without applying the operator to unit
vectors.  The stored tensors $T^j$ are the point values $\Sq$, with $a_j=b_j=q$ the point, or on TET10
the moments $\Mab$, with $(a_j,b_j)=(a,b)$ and the derivatives taken at the vertices as in
\eqref{eq:tet10moments}; $\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)$ is the position of a component among the $45$
stored ones.  It is stored as the
upper triangle, $3N(3N+1)/2$ numbers, and applied as a dense symmetric product, $(3N)^2$
multiply--adds.

\paragraph{The difference form.}  An element matrix of a gradient-based operator annihilates rigid
translations exactly: each diagonal $3\times3$ node block is minus the sum of the off-diagonal
blocks of its row.  Storing only the off-diagonal blocks $K_{pq}$ that couple nodes $p<q$, and
applying, with $h_q$ the increment at node $q$ and $y_p$ the output at node $p$,
\begin{equation}
  y_p \;=\; \sum_{q\ne p} K_{pq}\,(h_q - h_p)
  \label{eq:difference}
\end{equation}
saves the diagonal and, more importantly, keeps the translations in the kernel of the
\emph{encoded} operator whatever the rounding: the encoding error multiplies differences of the
increment, not the increment, which is the property Section~\ref{sec:errormodel} identifies as the
one that matters for smooth modes.  The differences are formed in the vector's precision before the
contraction, as for the gradients of the quadrature-point schedules.  The form is that of edge-based
schemes \cite{Lohner2008}; here it serves the encoding.

The property needs nothing of the stored blocks but $\tilde K_{qp}=\tilde K_{pq}^{\mathsf T}$.  The
basis is a partition of unity, $\sum_j\varphi_j=1$, so a rigid translation, $h_q=t$ at every node, has
zero gradient at every point and $\mathcal Ht=0$ by \eqref{eq:action}; hence
$K_{pp}=-\sum_{q\ne p}K_{pq}$, and the exact element matrix has the form \eqref{eq:difference}.  Let
$\tilde{\mathcal H}$ apply \eqref{eq:difference} with the decoded blocks $\tilde K_{pq}$ of any
encoding.  For a translation every difference $h_q-h_p$ vanishes, so $\tilde{\mathcal H}t=0$ whatever
the blocks, and exactly in floating-point arithmetic, since $h_q$ and $h_p$ are the same numbers.  The
encoding error acts only through the differences, $(\Delta h)_p=\sum_{q\ne p}(\tilde K_{pq}-K_{pq})
(h_q-h_p)$.

Encoding the full matrix instead gives $(\tilde{\mathcal H}t)_p=\sum_q(\tilde K_{pq}-K_{pq})\,t$,
the sum of a row's encoding errors, which rounding each block on its own does not cancel.
Infinitesimal rotations, $h_q=\omega\times x_q$, have the differences $\omega\times(x_q-x_p)$, so the
encoding error reaches them; the exact tangent annihilates them only at a stress-free state
(Section~\ref{sec:errormodel}).

\paragraph{A scale per node pair.}  One int8 scale per element spends the code range on the
largest block: the blocks $K_{pq}$ of one element differ in magnitude by orders on higher-order
elements (vertex--vertex against vertex--midside couplings).  Each pair therefore carries a factor
$r_{pq}\le1$ relative to the element scale $s_e$, an 8-bit float with five exponent and three
mantissa bits (Figure~\ref{fig:codecs}d), $r=2^{-E}(1+m/8)$, rounded up so that the pair's largest entry still fits the
largest code:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  K_{pq} \;\approx\; s_e\, r_{pq}\, C_{pq},\\
  r_{pq} \;=\; \min\bigl\{\, r = 2^{-E}(1+m/8) : r \ge \max|K_{pq}| / \max_{p'q'}|K_{p'q'}| \,\bigr\},
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:pairscale}
\end{equation}
with $C_{pq}$ the nine int8 codes.  The three mantissa bits bound the code range lost to the
rounding of $r$ by $1/8$; a power of two alone loses up to half of it.  The factor multiplies the
three differences of~\eqref{eq:difference} before the contraction (three multiplications per pair),
and $s_e$ the outputs, as before.  It costs one byte per nine codes.  As in the microscaling formats
of~\cite{Rouhani2023} the scale is shared by a small block; here the block is the node pair, the unit the
difference form already applies.  Figure~\ref{fig:fmtelement} summarises the stored form.

\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Difference form and symmetry.}  The rows of the assembled tangent annihilate the rigid
translations as the element matrices do, so \eqref{eq:difference} holds with $p$ and $q$ ranging over
the nodes: the diagonal blocks are neither stored nor indexed, and the translations stay in the kernel
of the encoded matrix by the argument of Section~\ref{sec:elementmatrix}, which uses only the form.  Encoding the full matrix does not survive the Newton study: in \texttt{int8}
$\fullEightFailed$ of the $\fullEightTried$ runs we made with it failed in the first load step.  Each
pair $i<j$ is stored once: with $d=h_j-h_i$, the block $K_{ij}$ adds $K_{ij}d$ to $y_i$ and
$-K_{ij}^{\mathsf T}d$ to $y_j$.  The off-diagonal blocks of the encoded matrix are then symmetric
by construction, $\tilde K_{ji}=\tilde K_{ij}^{\mathsf T}$, which the assembled ones are only to the
rounding of its summation order --- a difference that quantization can turn into a whole code step.
The implied diagonal blocks, $-\sum_{j\ne i}\tilde K_{ij}$, sum independently rounded blocks and are
symmetric only to the encoding error, as in the element difference form.  Storing each pair once
costs the operations of the full difference form from half of
its bytes.

\paragraph{Layout and application.}  The rows are held in the SELL-$C$-$\sigma$
format~\cite{Kreutzer2014}: $C=16$ rows side by side, sorted by length within windows of
$\sigma=32C$ rows and padded to the longest row of each group, so that one block position of the
$16$ rows is decoded as one vector, as in the element stores.  Columns are stored as $16$-bit offsets
$j-i$, positive in the upper triangle.  Each thread owns a contiguous range of whole windows and
updates $y_j$ of its own rows directly, lane after lane, since two rows of a group may share a
neighbour.  Blocks whose column lies beyond the range, or beyond the reach of the offsets, go to a
per-thread remainder with full indices; their updates of other threads' rows are collected in slots
and added after a barrier in a fixed order.  The product is deterministic and needs no atomics;
symmetric products have otherwise been parallelised by colouring~\cite{Alappat2020,Elafrou2019}.  At
$10^7$ degrees of freedom on $72$ threads the remainder holds $\remainderShareLo$--$\remainderShareHi\%$
of the stored blocks, padding included.  Algorithm~\ref{alg:qbsr} lists the product of one thread.

\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{Application of the quantized assembled matrix (difference form, each pair stored once),
one thread}
\label{alg:qbsr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the thread's rows $R_t$ (whole windows) and their groups of $C$ rows, lane $l$ holding row
  $i_l$; per step of a group and lane, the offset $o_l=j_l-i_l$ of the stored block $K_{i_lj_l}$, its
  codes $c_l$ ($3\times3$) and, in \texttt{int8}, its scale $s_l$; the thread's remainder
  $\mathcal{R}_t$ of blocks $(i,j,c,s)$ with full indices, and a slot $\theta$ for each whose column
  $j$ lies outside $R_t$; input $x$ and output $y$, three entries per node
\Constants $C$; the arithmetic type $\tau$; the matrix scale $\mu$ (\texttt{fp16}; in \texttt{int8}
  $\mu=1$ and the scale is per block)
\Ensure $y \gets y + \widetilde{K}x$, without atomics and in a fixed order of summation
\State $z_\theta \gets 0$ for the thread's slots \Comment{$z_\theta\in\R^3$}
\ForAll{groups of the thread}
  \State $a_l \gets 0$ for $l=1,\dots,C$ \Comment{the rows' sums}
  \For{each step of the group}
    \State $j_l \gets i_l + o_l$;\quad $d_l \gets \tau(x_{j_l} - x_{i_l})\, s_l$
           \Comment{one vector over the lanes}
    \State $a_l \gets a_l + c_l\, d_l$;\quad $t_l \gets c_l^{\mathsf T} d_l$
           \Comment{codes widened to $\tau$ in registers}
    \For{$l = 1,\dots,C$ in order} \Comment{two rows of a group may share a neighbour}
      \State $y_{j_l} \gets y_{j_l} - \mu\, t_l$ \Comment{$j_l\in R_t$}
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State $y_{i_l} \gets y_{i_l} + \mu\, a_l$ for each lane holding a row \Comment{padding has zero codes}
\EndFor
\ForAll{$(i,j,c,s)\in\mathcal{R}_t$ in order}
  \State $d \gets \tau(x_j - x_i)\, s\,\mu$;\quad $y_i \gets y_i + c\, d$
  \State \textbf{if} $j\in R_t$: $y_j \gets y_j - c^{\mathsf T}d$; \textbf{else} $z_\theta \gets z_\theta - c^{\mathsf T}d$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{barrier} \Comment{all threads}
\ForAll{slots $\theta$ of all threads whose row $i_\theta$ lies in $R_t$, in a fixed order}
  \State $y_{i_\theta} \gets y_{i_\theta} + z_\theta$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

\paragraph{Set-up and memory.}  The encoder writes the encoded blocks over the assembled values: a
stored block takes $13$ bytes in \texttt{int8} ($18$ in \texttt{fp16}) against $36$ for an assembled
one, and only the upper blocks are stored.  It proceeds in rounds, each covering the windows whose
encoded blocks end before the first value still to be read.  The single-precision values stay
allocated for the next assembly, so the peak memory follows that of single-precision BSR; the
encoded store is what the application reads.  Figure~\ref{fig:fmtqbsr} summarises the format.

\section{Kernels on the CPU}
\label{sec:kernels}

The set-up and the applications of the element formats traverse the mesh in blocks of $V$ elements,
one element per vector lane, and every statement in Algorithms~\ref{alg:setup}--\ref{alg:applyelement}
is a vector operation over the lanes: the lane loop is innermost and is not written.  The quantized
matrix has a traversal of its own (Algorithm~\ref{alg:qbsr}).  Constants of the reference element ---
quadrature points and weights, the tabulated derivatives $\partial_m\varphi_j(\xhat_q)$, the vertex
derivatives $G$ of \eqref{eq:tet10moments}, the barycentric values $\lambda_a(\xhat_q)$ --- are
compile-time literals.

\paragraph{Notation.}  The mesh has $n_e$ elements of $N$ nodes and $n_n$ nodes; $E(e,p)$ is
the global node of local node $p$ of element $e$, and vectors are indexed by node and component.
$\mathcal{C}$ is the storage type of the encoding --- \texttt{int8} or \texttt{fp16}, or
\texttt{fp32}/\texttt{fp64} for the uncompressed references.  An element stores $n_v$ tensors $T_1,\dots,T_{n_v}$ of $45$ packed
components each: the point tangents $\Sq$ ($n_v=n_q$), or the moments $\Mab$, $a\le b$
($n_v=10$ on TET10).  Each carries a reference weight $X_j$ --- $w_q$ for a point, $X_{ab}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:mean} for a moment --- with which the element mean is
$\Sbar = \sum_j T_j/|\refcell|$ and the deviations are $T_j - X_j\Sbar$.  On the hexahedra the
fold of Section~\ref{sec:fold} leaves point $j$ the components $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_j$; a
component it receives from the dropped point $j'$ of its line with weight $\ell$ has the reference
weight $X_{j\alpha}=X_j+\ell X_{j'}$, and $X_{j\alpha}=X_j$ otherwise.  Elsewhere $\mathcal{A}_j$ is
all $45$ components.  The component $\alpha$ of
a packed tensor holds $T_{ikmn}$ for $\alpha=\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)$, the upper-triangle index of
the pair $\bigl((i,n),(k,m)\bigr)$ of Section~\ref{sec:formulation}; read as a $9\times9$
symmetric matrix on that pairing, a tensor acts on the nine-vector of a gradient indexed by
$(k,m)$.  The encoded value of
component $\alpha$ of tensor $j$ is $c_{j\alpha}$, the block's scales are $s$ (per element) or
$s_\alpha$ (per component), and $\tau$ is the arithmetic type of the contraction.  Decoding returns
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{dec}(c_{j\alpha}) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tau(c_{j\alpha}) & \text{\texttt{fp16} scaled, \texttt{int8} per element}\\
                      & \text{\quad (scale on the outputs),}\\
    s_\alpha\,\tau(c_{j\alpha}) & \text{\texttt{int8} per component,}\\
    X_{j\alpha}\,\Sbar_\alpha + s\,\tau(c_{j\alpha}) & \text{\texttt{int8} mean and deviation,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
Only the first case leaves a scale to be applied;
because the action is linear in the store, it multiplies the element's outputs once.

\subsection{Data layout}
\label{sec:layout}

A store of $45$ numbers per element can be held as $45$ separate arrays over the elements, each read
with unit stride across the vector lanes, with a padded stride between them to keep the $45$ streams
out of the same cache sets.  At $n_q$ points the same layout becomes $45\,n_q$ streams --- $\perpointHexEight$ on
HEX8 --- which exceeds what hardware prefetchers track and makes the set conflicts systematic.  We
therefore also consider a blocked layout that keeps the vectorisation and restores a single stream:
for a block of $V$ elements (one vector of lanes), all of its encoded numbers are contiguous, ordered
point, component, lane.  Each component of each point is still a unit-stride vector load of $V$
values, which for \texttt{int8} and \texttt{fp16} is a narrow load followed by a widening
conversion, and a block's scales are stored with it.  The element-major order also lets the encoder
write a block in one pass (Figure~\ref{fig:fmtpoints}).  Both layouts are measured.

\subsection{Set-up}
\label{sec:setupkernel}

\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{Set-up: evaluate, reduce and encode the store (once per Newton step)}
\label{alg:setup}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require connectivity $E\in\mathbb{N}^{n_e\times N}$; reference coordinates $x\in\R^{3n_n}$;
  current state $u\in\R^{3n_n}$; material parameters $\theta$ (uniform, or one set per element)
\Constants rule $(\xhat_q,w_q)_{q\le n_q}$; derivatives $\nabla\varphi_j(\xhat_q)$; for moments,
  $\lambda_a(\xhat_q)$ and $X_{ab}$; $|\refcell|$; the encoding (storage type, scale granularity,
  plain or mean) and the representation (points or moments)
\Ensure encoded store $c\in\mathcal{C}^{n_e\times n_v\times45}$ in the block layout; scales
  $s\in\R^{n_e}$ (per element) or $s\in\R^{n_e\times45}$ (per component); for mean and deviation the
  means $\Sbar\in\R^{n_e\times45}$ in single precision
\ForAll{blocks $B$ of $V$ elements \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State gather $x$ and $u$ at the nodes $E(B,\cdot)$ of the block
  \State $T_j \gets 0$ for $j=1,\dots,n_v$;\quad $\Sbar \gets 0$
  \For{$q = 1,\dots,n_q$}
    \State $J \gets \sum_j x_j \otimes \nabla\varphi_j(\xhat_q)$;\quad
           $F \gets I + \sum_j u_j \otimes \nabla\varphi_j(\xhat_q)\, J^{-1}$
    \State $A \gets \partial P/\partial F\,(F;\theta)$ \Comment{the material law}
    \State $\Sq \gets w_q\,\mathrm{pullback}(A, J^{-1}, \det J)$ \Comment{\eqref{eq:pullback}, $45$ components}
    \State $\Sbar \gets \Sbar + \Sq$
    \If{the store holds points}
      \State $T_q \gets \Sq$
    \Else
      \ForAll{$a \le b$}
        $T_{ab} \gets T_{ab} + \lambda_a(\xhat_q)\lambda_b(\xhat_q)\,\Sq$
      \EndFor
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $\Sbar \gets \Sbar/|\refcell|$
  \If{mean and deviation}
    \State write $\Sbar$ in single precision;\quad $T_j \gets T_j - X_j\Sbar$
  \EndIf
  \If{the store is folded}
    \State $T_{j\alpha} \gets T_{j\alpha} + \ell\,T_{j'\alpha}$ for $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_j$ received from
           $j'$ \Comment{\eqref{eq:fold}; exact}
  \EndIf
  \State $\sigma \gets \max_{j,\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_j}|T_{j\alpha}|$ \textbf{or}
         $\sigma_\alpha \gets \max_j |T_{j\alpha}|$ \Comment{per element or per component}
  \State $s \gets \mathrm{scale}(\sigma)$ \Comment{\texttt{int8}: $\sigma/127$;
         \texttt{fp16}: $2^{\lceil\log_2\sigma\rceil-14}$}
  \State $c_{j\alpha} \gets \mathrm{encode}(T_{j\alpha}/s)$ for all $j$, $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_j$;\quad write $c$ and $s$
         \Comment{block layout: $j$, $\alpha$, lane}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:setup} keeps the block's $n_v$ tensors in scratch until its scale is known, so
encoding is a second pass over data still in cache rather than over memory, and no full-precision
copy of the store exists outside the block.  The element matrices take the block's tensors through one
more contraction, \eqref{eq:elementmatrix}, in the same scratch: vectorised over the lanes, it forms only
the blocks $K_{pq}$ above the diagonal, then the element scale, the pair factors \eqref{eq:pairscale}
and the codes as above.

\subsection{Application schedules}
\label{sec:schedules}

Without a symbolic reference tensor there is nothing to integrate at code-generation time, but
the rank structure of the gradient spaces survives and determines the schedule.  Three schedules
are considered; their operation counts, after common-subexpression elimination with the stored
numbers as opaque inputs, are in Table~\ref{tab:flops}.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item[Direct, per point.]  For each point, form the $3\times3$ reference gradient of the increment
  from tabulated basis derivatives, apply the decoded $9\times9$ symmetric tangent ($81$
  multiply--adds), and scatter through the transposed table.  Eliminating the gradient and
  scatter stages jointly across points recovers the sharing of a tensor-product element, which on
  HEX8 is what sum factorisation would make explicit.
\item[Modal, per point.]  Compress the increment once into its $9r$ modal gradient coefficients
  --- the coefficients of its reference gradient in a basis of the $r$-dimensional gradient space
  of each direction, through a map $C$ whose rational constants are folded into the code ---;
  evaluate each point's gradient from the modes; apply the tangent; accumulate back to modes;
  expand with $C^{\mathsf T}$.  On TET10 the
  modes are the vertex derivatives and the evaluation is barycentric interpolation.
\item[Moment.]  Equation~\eqref{eq:tet10moments}: compress, apply the $r^2$ blocks $\Mab$ to the
  modal coefficients, expand.
\end{description}

On every element the dominant term is the tangent contraction, $81$ multiply--adds per point or

(Section~\ref{sec:fold}).  It is not reducible by symmetry, since a symmetric matrix--vector product still touches every entry, and it is
the reason the arithmetic precision is a design parameter (Section~\ref{sec:precision}).

\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{Application, moment schedule (TET10; TET4 with $r=1$)}
\label{alg:applymoments}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require connectivity $E\in\mathbb{N}^{n_e\times N}$; increment $h\in\R^{3n_n}$; the outputs of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:setup}: $c$, $s$, and $\Sbar$ where the encoding has it; output vector
  $y\in\R^{3n_n}$
\Constants vertex derivatives $G_{jma}$ ($j\le N$, $m\le3$, $a\le r$); $X_{ab}$; $\mathrm{slot}$;
  the encoding and the arithmetic type $\tau$
\Ensure $y \gets y + \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}h$, accumulated in place
  \Comment{no geometry, state or material read}
\ForAll{blocks $B$ of $V$ elements \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State gather $h$ at the nodes $E(B,\cdot)$ \Comment{vector precision}
  \State $P_{akm} \gets \sum_j G_{jma}\, h_{kj}$ for $a\le r$, $k,m\le 3$
         \Comment{vertex derivatives; folded constants}
  \State $P \gets \tau(P)$
  \State $Y_{bin} \gets 0$
  \For{$a \le b$}
    \State $\widehat M_\alpha \gets \mathrm{dec}(c_{ab,\alpha})$, $\alpha=1,\dots,45$
           \Comment{\eqref{eq:decode}, in registers}
    \State $Y_{bin} \gets Y_{bin} + \sum_{k,m} \widehat M_{\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)}\, P_{akm}$
           \Comment{$81$ multiply--adds}
    \If{$a \ne b$}
      \State $Y_{ain} \gets Y_{ain} + \sum_{k,m} \widehat M_{\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)}\, P_{bkm}$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathrm{out}_{ip} \gets \sum_{b,n} G_{pnb}\, Y_{bin}$ \Comment{vector precision}
  \State \textbf{if} the scale is on the outputs: $\mathrm{out} \gets s\,\mathrm{out}$
  \State $y_{E(B,p),i} \gets y_{E(B,p),i} + \mathrm{out}_{ip}$ \Comment{atomic, or race-free when packed}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:applymoments} decodes each stored moment once and applies it to both
$(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$, so each of the ten tensors is read and converted once while all $r^2=16$
blocks of the contraction of \eqref{eq:tet10moments} are performed.  The first and last stages
compress the increment into the vertex derivatives and expand the result, with their rational
constants folded into the code; the middle stage performs the sixteen block contractions.

\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{Application, per-point schedule (HEX8)}
\label{alg:applypoints}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require connectivity $E\in\mathbb{N}^{n_e\times N}$; increment $h\in\R^{3n_n}$; the outputs of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:setup}: $c$, $s$, and $\Sbar$ where the encoding has it; output vector
  $y\in\R^{3n_n}$
\Constants derivatives $\partial_m\varphi_j(\xhat_q)$ (as one-dimensional factors); $w_q$;
  $\mathrm{slot}$; the encoding and the arithmetic type $\tau$
\Ensure $y \gets y + \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}h$, accumulated in place
  \Comment{no geometry, state or material read}
\ForAll{blocks $B$ of $V$ elements \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State gather $h$ at the nodes $E(B,\cdot)$ \Comment{vector precision}
  \State $g^q_{km} \gets \sum_j \partial_m\varphi_j(\xhat_q)\, h_{kj}$ for all $q$
         \Comment{one-dimensional sweeps shared across points}
  \State $g \gets \tau(g)$
  \For{$q = 1,\dots,n_q$}
    \State $\widehat S_\alpha \gets \mathrm{dec}(c_{q\alpha})$, $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_q$
           \Comment{\eqref{eq:decode}, in registers}
    \State $y^q_{in} \gets \sum_{k,m} \widehat S_{\mathrm{slot}(i,k,m,n)}\, g^q_{km}$ over the stored slots
           \Comment{$81-9d_q$ multiply--adds}
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathrm{out}_{ip} \gets \sum_q \sum_n \partial_n\varphi_p(\xhat_q)\, y^q_{in}$
         \Comment{transposed sweeps; vector precision}
  \State \textbf{if} the scale is on the outputs: $\mathrm{out} \gets s\,\mathrm{out}$
  \State $y_{E(B,p),i} \gets y_{E(B,p),i} + \mathrm{out}_{ip}$ \Comment{atomic, or race-free when packed}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:applypoints} the gradient and output stages are eliminated jointly across
the points, which on a tensor-product element is what sum factorisation makes explicit: the
derivative in one direction is a difference of nodal values interpolated in the other two, and the
interpolations are shared between directions.  The point loop holds one decoded tangent at a time,
at most $45$ values in registers, with the nine-entry gradient and output of that point; $d_q$ is
the number of directions in which point $q$ is the dropped point of its line.  In both
application kernels the only per-element data read are the encoded store, its scales (and, for the
mean-and-deviation encodings, the $45$-entry single-precision mean), the
connectivity and the increment; geometry, state and material parameters are not read.

\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{Application, element matrix in difference form}
\label{alg:applyelement}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require connectivity $E\in\mathbb{N}^{n_e\times N}$; increment $h\in\R^{3n_n}$; per element the codes
  $C_{pq}\in\mathcal{C}^{3\times3}$ of each node pair $p<q$, its factor $r_{pq}$ (\texttt{int8} with a
  factor per pair; $r_{pq}=1$ otherwise) and the element scale $s_e$; output vector $y\in\R^{3n_n}$
\Constants the $N(N-1)/2$ node pairs; the arithmetic type $\tau$
\Ensure $y \gets y + \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}h$, accumulated in place \Comment{no geometry, state or material read}
\ForAll{blocks $B$ of $V$ elements \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State gather $h$ at the nodes $E(B,\cdot)$ \Comment{vector precision}
  \State $\mathrm{out}_p \gets 0$ for $p=1,\dots,N$
  \ForAll{pairs $p<q$}
    \State $d \gets r_{pq}\,\tau(h_q - h_p)$ \Comment{the difference in vector precision, then rounded}
    \State $K \gets \tau(C_{pq})$ \Comment{nine codes, widened in registers}
    \State $\mathrm{out}_p \gets \mathrm{out}_p + K d$;\quad $\mathrm{out}_q \gets \mathrm{out}_q - K^{\mathsf T} d$
           \Comment{\eqref{eq:difference}, both nodes of the pair}
  \EndFor
  \State $y_{E(B,p)} \gets y_{E(B,p)} + s_e\,\mathrm{out}_p$ \Comment{packed two-pass}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:applyelement} reads each stored block once and applies it to both nodes of its pair,
$18$ multiply--adds for nine codes, and forms no gradient: the element matrix trades the point store's
gradient and output stages for a larger store (Table~\ref{tab:elementmatrix}).  A translation gives
$d=0$ in every pair and leaves $y$ unchanged whatever the codes (Figure~\ref{fig:fmtelement}d).

\paragraph{The folded kernels.}  The set-up (Algorithm~\ref{alg:setup}) evaluates the tangent at all
$q_1^3$ points as before, folds the block's point values in scratch after the mean is removed, takes
the scale over the kept values and encodes only those, point by point in the store's order.  The
application (Algorithm~\ref{alg:applypoints}) decodes the components a point keeps into their slots
and contracts with one of eight generated contractions, one per set of dropped directions.  Since
the vector lanes are elements and every element drops the same components at the same point, the
set is uniform across the lanes and the skipped multiply--adds are saved.  On Grace the folded
application is faster than the one over all $45\,n_q$ point values for every encoding, by
$\foldGainHalfHexEight\%$ (\texttt{fp16}) and $\foldGainEightMdHexEight\%$ (\texttt{int8} mean and
deviation) on HEX8 and $\foldGainHalfHexTwentySeven\%$ and $\foldGainEightMdHexTwentySeven\%$ on HEX27
at about $10^7$ degrees of freedom; selecting the contraction per point at run time measured faster
than unrolling all points with their sets fixed at compile time.

\subsection{Arithmetic precision}
\label{sec:precision}

The stored numbers carry at most eleven significant bits (\texttt{fp16}) or eight (\texttt{int8}),
so carrying the contraction in double precision buys nothing: single-precision arithmetic
contributes a relative error of $2^{-24}$, three orders of magnitude below either encoding, and
doubles the vector width.  The gradient stage is kept in the vector's precision, because it forms
differences of nodal values whose common part can be large compared with the increment's
variation over the element; the gradients are rounded to single precision only after the
differences are formed.  Outputs are accumulated in the vector's precision.  The arithmetic type is
a template parameter of the kernels, independent of the storage type, so both precisions remain
measurable.

\subsection{The packed two-pass traversal}
\label{sec:packed}

An element operator adds its outputs to nodes that neighbouring elements share, which the threads of
a parallel loop must not update concurrently.  Atomic additions resolve the conflict but make the
result depend on the order of the threads.  The element formats are therefore applied through the
packed layout of our code \cite{PackedMesh} (Figure~\ref{fig:packed}).  Elements are grouped into
packs whose nodes are numbered locally in $16$ bits.  Each node is owned by one pack, and the nodes a
pack owns form a contiguous range of the global numbering; the nodes it shares with the packs that own
them are its ghosts.  The first pass runs the packs as the iterations of a parallel loop: a pack
gathers the increment at its owned nodes and its ghosts into node-major scratch of its thread, its
blocks of $V$ elements read the scratch through the local indices and accumulate into it, and the pack
adds its owned nodes into the result, which no other pack writes, and its ghost contributions into
slots of their own.  The second pass sums, for each node with slots, its slots in a fixed order.
Neither pass uses atomics, so the result is bitwise reproducible.  The scratch keeps a node's
components in one cache line for the gather and the scatter, and each thread keeps it across packs and
applications; the local indices halve the connectivity an element reads against $32$-bit global ones.

\section{Kernels on the GPU}
\label{sec:gpukernels}

The device keeps the stores of the CPU and maps its threads to them differently
(Figure~\ref{fig:gpu}).  The compressed stores keep their host layouts with blocks of $32$ elements,
one warp, and are set up on the device: the codes equal the host's bit for bit, except a handful of
element-matrix codes on TET10 that differ by one rounding step.

\paragraph{A thread per element.}  On TET4, TET10 and HEX8 the application runs one thread per
element, so a warp holds the $32$ elements of a store block and every code load of the blocked layout
is coalesced.  The thread adds its outputs with atomic additions, or on TET4 and in half precision on
HEX8 through a block-local scatter, whichever measured faster: the block's distinct nodes are read
into shared memory, its elements gather and accumulate there, and the block adds each of its nodes into
the result once.  The HEX8 point store is folded as on Grace, and its threads skip the same terms.

\paragraph{A thread per point.}  A HEX27 element has $64$ points and $27$ nodes, more state than one
thread holds.  Its point store runs a thread per quadrature point, two warps per element, with the
sum-factorised schedule: the element's increment is staged in shared memory, the gradients at the
points are formed by one-dimensional sweeps along the lines of points that the element's threads run
together, each thread decodes and contracts its own point, and the transposed sweeps return the
outputs.  The set-up maps its threads alike.  This store keeps all $\perpointHexTwentySeven$ point
values: the threads of a warp would drop different directions, a warp executes one instruction stream,
so the dropped terms would be multiplied by zero rather than skipped; the fold would save only a tenth
of the bytes, which do not bound this kernel, while its addressing and folded weights cost
instructions and registers.

\paragraph{A warp per element.}  The HEX27 element matrix runs one warp per element, a lane per node:
lane $p$ reads the increment from shared memory, forms its row of the difference form
\eqref{eq:difference} with the pair factors, and adds it to its node.

\paragraph{The quantized matrix.}  On the device the quantized matrix is held in the full difference
form, each pair of blocks stored twice: storing a pair once needs a scatter of the transposed block,
which costs more than the bytes it saves.  Its rows are in SELL-$32$-$1024$, a thread per row, with the
codes, column and scale of a block in one record, so each thread writes its own row once and no
atomics are needed.  Columns are full $32$-bit indices: $16$-bit offsets, as on Grace, store fewer bytes
but send the blocks out of their reach to a slower remainder kernel, and applied as
$y \leftarrow y + Ax$ they are slower on every mesh measured.

\paragraph{BSR.}  BSR is assembled by our code's device kernels from the same generator and applied by
its block product, a group of sixteen threads per block row whose partial sums are added by warp
shuffles in a fixed order.

\subsection{Time to solution}

Per Newton step the cost is $T_{\mathrm{setup}} + n_{\mathrm{it}}\,T_{\mathrm{apply}}$, and the
two representations differ in both terms.  The setup of the compressed store evaluates the point
tangents and writes the encoded store, a bandwidth cost of a few milliseconds at the sizes
considered.  A BSR setup computes the element matrices, which costs an additional
$O(N^2 d^2 n_q)$ contraction per element over the point tangents, and scatters them into a matrix
that is $\ratiointHexEight\times$ larger than the \texttt{int8} store.  The model predicts the
compressed operator ahead on setup by a large factor and level or ahead on application, so ahead
on time to solution whenever the encoding keeps the Newton and Krylov iteration counts of the exact
tangent --- the condition $\eps\ll\eta$ of Section~\ref{sec:newton}.

\subsection{Correctness}

application to round-off (\corrQpSixtyFourHexEight{} relative on HEX8, \corrQpSixtyFourTetFour{} on

the ten moments carry everything the quadrature sees.  In the Newton study the double-precision

the element matrices built from the store do the same.

\subsection{Element matrices}

The element matrices of Section~\ref{sec:elementmatrix} are built by the same set-up kernel and
applied through the same scatter, and they are the fastest element operators measured
(Figure~\ref{fig:throughput}).  In half precision and difference form they apply at
$\EaDiffHalfRateHexEight$ and $\EaDiffHalfRateTetTen$~MDOF/s on HEX8 and TET10,
$\EaDiffHalfVsThirtyTwoHexEight\times$ and $\EaDiffHalfVsThirtyTwoTetTen\times$ the speed of
single-precision BSR from $\EaDiffHalfMemVsThirtyTwoHexEight\times$ and
$\EaDiffHalfMemVsThirtyTwoTetTen\times$ less memory (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}); in \texttt{int8} the
factors grow to $\EaDiffEightVsThirtyTwoHexEight\times$ and $\EaDiffEightVsThirtyTwoTetTen\times$ in
speed.  The difference form is faster than the full matrix and, as designed, more accurate under
the same encoding (energy error \errEaDiffHalfHexEight{} against \errEaFullHalfHexEight{} on HEX8 in
half precision); in the Newton study its half-precision encoding keeps the exact iteration counts
where the full matrix in half precision does not ($\newtonEaFullHalfTetTen$ Newton iterations on
TET10 at about $10^6$ degrees of freedom, against $\newtonExactTetTen$).  Their set-up contracts the stored tensors per element, vectorised over the lanes of a
block of elements and computing only the blocks above the diagonal ($\SetupEaDiffHalfHexEight$~ms on
HEX8 against $\SetupQpHalfHexEight$ for the half-precision point store and
$\SetupBsrThirtyTwoHexEight$ for single-precision BSR assembly; $\SetupEaDiffHalfTetTen$,
$\SetupQpHalfTetTen$ and $\SetupBsrThirtyTwoTetTen$ on TET10): the contraction is where the element
matrix pays for its smaller application.  On TET4 the double-precision element matrices were
measured at $3.1\times10^6$~dof only; their bytes per degree of freedom enter
Figure~\ref{fig:memory}.

On HEX27 the element matrix is the larger store (Table~\ref{tab:elementmatrix}) and still the faster
operator: in half precision and difference form it applies at
$\EaDiffHalfVsThirtyTwoHexTwentySeven\times$ the speed of single-precision BSR, against
$\QpHalfVsThirtyTwoHexTwentySeven\times$ for the half-precision point store.  With one scale per element
\texttt{int8} spends its range on the largest node-pair block there: its energy error is
\errEaDiffEightHexTwentySeven{}, against \errEaPairEightHexTwentySeven{} with a scale per node pair
\eqref{eq:pairscale}, which costs $\pairSlowdownHexTwentySeven\%$ of the throughput.  On TET10 and
TET4 the pair scale divides the error by $\pairErrGainTetTen$ and $\pairErrGainTetFour$; on HEX8, whose
node pairs are of similar size, it gains nothing and costs $\pairSlowdownHexEight\%$.  The Newton
iteration shows the difference: at about $10^6$~dof the element-scaled \texttt{int8} matrix needs
$\newtonEaDiffEightHexTwentySeven$ Newton iterations on HEX27 and $\newtonEaDiffEightTetTen$ on TET10,
against $\newtonExactHexTwentySeven$ and $\newtonExactTetTen$ for the exact operators, the
pair-scaled one $\newtonEaPairEightHexTwentySeven$ and $\newtonEaPairEightTetTen$, and the
half-precision difference form keeps the exact counts.  Under the twist the pair scale restores the
exact count on HEX8 ($\twistEaPairEightHexEight$ against $\twistEaDiffEightHexEight$ with one scale per
element), and on HEX27, where the element-scaled matrix does not complete the twist, the pair-scaled
one takes $\twistEaPairEightHexTwentySeven$ iterations against $\twistExactHexTwentySeven$ for the
exact operator; there the counts of all formats, BSR's in double and single precision included,
spread by about a tenth.  On TET4 the pair scale does not help: both \texttt{int8} matrices need
about half as many iterations again as the exact $\twistExactTetFour$ ($\twistEaDiffEightTetFour$ and
$\twistEaPairEightTetFour$), although the pair scale divides TET4's white-noise error by
$\pairErrGainTetFour$ --- another instance of that measure not predicting the iteration --- and the
half-precision matrix remains the choice there.

\subsection{Quantized assembled matrices}

The quantized matrix of Section~\ref{sec:qbsr} is the fastest operator on three of the four
elements (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput}).  In \texttt{int8} it applies at $\QbsrEightVsThirtyTwoTetFour$,
$\QbsrEightVsThirtyTwoTetTen$, $\QbsrEightVsThirtyTwoHexEight$ and $\QbsrEightVsThirtyTwoHexTwentySeven\times$
the speed of single-precision BSR on TET4, TET10, HEX8 and HEX27, from
$\QbsrEightMemVsThirtyTwoTetTen$--$\QbsrEightMemVsThirtyTwoTetFour\times$ less memory
(Figure~\ref{fig:memory}), and in \texttt{fp16} at $\QbsrHalfVsThirtyTwoTetTen$--$\QbsrHalfVsThirtyTwoTetFour\times$.
Storing each pair once makes it $\upperGainHexTwentySeven$--$\upperGainHexEight\times$ faster than the
full difference form from $\upperMemTetTen$--$\upperMemTetFour\times$ less memory, and the column offsets
save a further $\offsetSavingHexEight\%$.  The product is not bandwidth-bound: it moves
$\gbsQbsrEightHexEight$~GB/s on HEX8 against $\gbsBsrThirtyTwoHexEight$ for single-precision BSR, since
every stored block reads its own $h_j$, which an element operator reads once per element node.  On
HEX27, whose rows have the most blocks, the element matrix with a scale per node pair stays ahead
($\EaPairEightRateHexTwentySeven$ against $\QbsrEightRateHexTwentySeven$~MDOF/s).  Its set-up is BSR's
assembly plus the encoding ($\SetupQbsrEightHexEight$ against $\SetupBsrThirtyTwoHexEight$~ms on HEX8).

In the Newton study the \texttt{fp16} matrix keeps the iteration counts of BSR at about $10^6$
degrees of freedom ($\newtonQbsrHalfHexEight$, $\newtonQbsrHalfTetTen$ and $\newtonQbsrHalfHexTwentySeven$
on HEX8, TET10 and HEX27, one more on TET4) and under the twist on the tetrahedra
($\twistQbsrHalfTetTen$ on TET10, the exact count); at about $10^7$ on TET10 it needs
$\tenmNewtonQbsrHalfTetTen$ against $\tenmNewtonExactTetTen$, the threshold the half-precision point
store meets there.
\texttt{int8} with a scale per block needs about a third more ($\newtonQbsrEightTetTen$ against
$\newtonExactTetTen$ on TET10 at $10^6$), as the element matrix with a scale per node pair.  At about
$10^7$ degrees of freedom the \texttt{fp16} matrix solves in $\timeQbsrHalfTetFour$, $\timeQbsrHalfTetTen$,
$\timeQbsrHalfHexEight$ and $\timeQbsrHalfHexTwentySeven\times$ the time of single-precision BSR on TET4,
TET10, HEX8 and HEX27, and the \texttt{int8} matrix in $\timeQbsrEightTetFour$, $\timeQbsrEightTetTen$,
$\timeQbsrEightHexEight$ and $\timeQbsrEightHexTwentySeven\times$ (Figure~\ref{fig:tts}): the fastest
operator measured on the tetrahedra, level with the element matrices on HEX8
($\timeEaPairEightHexEight\times$ with a scale per node pair), and behind them on HEX27
($\timeEaPairEightHexTwentySeven\times$).

The application reads $\storeQbsrEightHexEight$~GiB on HEX8 at $\peakDofHexEight$ degrees of freedom,
but the peak memory of the whole Newton run is $\peakQbsrEightHexEight$~GiB, against
$\peakBsrThirtyTwoHexEight$ for single-precision BSR and $\peakEaPairEightHexEight$ for the \texttt{int8}
element matrix with a scale per node pair (on TET4: $\peakQbsrEightTetFour$, $\peakBsrThirtyTwoTetFour$
and $\peakEaPairEightTetFour$~GiB).  The single-precision assembly and the node graph it is built on set
the peak, not the store; assembling and encoding the matrix in chunks of rows would remove the first.

$\gpuBsrThirtyTwoGbsTetTen$), and the element formats are ahead of it on every mesh, unlike on the
TET4 meshes, at $\appTenGpuQpHalfVsGivenMin$--$\appTenGpuEaDiffHalfVsGivenMax\times$ its speed,
the \texttt{int8} quantized matrix at $\appTenGpuQbsrEightVsGivenMin$--$\appTenGpuQbsrEightVsGivenMax\times$
(on the cube $\gpuQbsrEightVsThirtyTwoTetTen\times$).  The quantized matrix leads on the two larger
meshes; on the two smaller ones the half-precision element matrix applies fastest.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The choice of format.}  It follows the element (Table~\ref{tab:summary}), and on the
quadratic elements the machine (Section~\ref{sec:gpu}).  On Grace, where a matrix
row has few blocks and the element formats repeat each coupling in every element that shares it ---
linear and quadratic tetrahedra --- the quantized assembled matrix is the fastest format.  Where
rows are long and elements large --- triquadratic hexahedra --- the element formats win, because they
gather each node once per element where the matrix gathers it once per block.  Trilinear hexahedra
sit between.  Across all elements the element formats keep the lowest peak memory, since they need
neither a single-precision assembly nor the node graph; assembling and encoding the matrix in chunks
of rows would close part of that gap.

\paragraph{Where the compressed operators pay.}  Their advantage is bandwidth, so it appears
where the sparse matrix streams from memory (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}).  In Newton time to solution
on one Grace CPU the compressed operators beat single-precision BSR on HEX27, whose matrix has the
most blocks per row, from a few $10^5$ degrees of freedom; on HEX8 and TET10 the half-precision
element matrix does from about $10^6$, while the point stores draw level with single-precision BSR
on HEX8 at $10^7$ and stay behind it on TET10.  Below that the matrix lives in cache and BSR is
two to three times faster than its bandwidth bound; no element operator, compressed or not,
competes there.  In a distributed run this is a statement about the problem size per node.  The
set-up, where the compressed stores are cheapest, is a small part of a Newton step at the Krylov
tolerances used here; it matters more when the Krylov iteration is short.

\paragraph{On the GPU.}  The bytes decide less than on Grace for the element formats.  The BSR
product runs at the memory bandwidth ($\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMin$--$\gpuBsrSixtyFourPctMax\%$ of the
H100's nominal in double precision); the quantized matrix, which streams its records as BSR
streams its values, turns its byte saving into speed as on Grace or better, while the element
formats, held back by their gathers, atomic scatters and per-element latency, keep least of theirs,
and on triquadratic hexahedra the element matrices fall to the speed of BSR or below.  The set-up weighs more: the
assembly that the quantized matrix shares with BSR speeds up less than the products, so where it is
expensive --- quadratic tetrahedra and triquadratic hexahedra --- the quadrature-point stores, whose
set-up is about $\gpuTenmSetupShareQpHalfTetTen\%$ of the run, solve fastest.  On linear tetrahedra and
trilinear hexahedra the quantized matrix remains the choice.


\paragraph{Points or element matrices.}  At the orders studied the element matrix is no larger than
the point store on HEX8 and TET10 and cheaper to apply, so it is the better compressed
object there, provided its set-up is as efficient as the point store's.  The point store wins on
TET4, whose single point is smaller than its matrix, and wherever the element matrix grows faster
than the quadrature --- with polynomial degree, since the matrix grows as the square of the node
count.  HEX27 is past the crossover in memory but not in operations: its element matrix is a tenth
larger than the point store and still needs fewer operations than the sum-factorised point schedule,
and it is the faster operator on Grace; on the H100, where its per-element work weighs more, the
point store is.

\paragraph{What the error measure must see.}  A relative error per stored number, or an operator
error measured against white noise, does not predict the effect on Newton's method; the iteration
counts do.  The modes that decide it are smooth and locally nearly rigid, where the element operator
is close to singular: an encoding whose absolute error there is set by the largest entry of the
tangent costs iterations, one whose error is set by the variation of the tangent over the element,
or which keeps the rigid translations exact, does not.

\paragraph{Limitations.}  The study uses one material, structured meshes for the Newton runs (the
application benchmark also on unstructured TET4 and TET10 meshes, Section~\ref{sec:appmesh}), and a Krylov method
without preconditioner, chosen so that every operator runs the same iteration; with a
preconditioner built from the operator, the encodings change the preconditioner too.  Materials
defined by a residual carry $81$ numbers per point.  The packed traversal is sensitive to the number
of packs per thread, which our code's default pack size leaves too low below about $10^6$
degrees of freedom on 72 threads; the measurements set it for every operator alike.

\section{Conclusions}

Memory is saved by one set of measures in two steps, for every format.  Exact reductions come
first and cost no accuracy: the symmetry of the tangent, a store sized to what the quadrature can
see --- moments on tetrahedra, point values folded along the lines of quadrature points on
hexahedra --- and the difference form remove $\exactQpPctHexEight\%$ of the point store,
$\exactEaPctHexEight\%$ of the element matrix and $\exactBsrPctHexEight\%$ of the assembled matrix
on trilinear hexahedra, and the fold also removes arithmetic.  Encodings then divide what is left by
two to eight, and only they perturb the tangent.

Which compressed format of the tangent pays in a Newton--Krylov hyperelasticity solve depends on the
element, and two properties decide which encodings are usable at all.  The rounding must follow the
stored values or their variation over the element, not the largest entry of the element: half
precision and \texttt{int8} deviations from the element mean keep the exact iteration counts,
\texttt{int8} with one scale per element does not.  And an encoded element or assembled matrix must
keep the rigid translations in its kernel, which the difference form guarantees whatever the
rounding; it is what makes \texttt{int8} usable for both.  On one Grace CPU at $10^7$ degrees of
freedom the quantized assembled matrix is the fastest format on linear and quadratic tetrahedra, the
element matrices come close to it on trilinear hexahedra and lead on triquadratic ones, and the
element formats keep the lowest peak memory.  On Grace all of them need the operator to stream from
memory: while the matrix fits in the last-level cache, BSR is faster.  On one H100 the quantized matrix leads on linear
tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra and the quadrature-point stores on quadratic tetrahedra and
triquadratic hexahedra, where the assembly the matrices need weighs more than on the CPU.
