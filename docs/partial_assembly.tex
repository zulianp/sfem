\begin{abstract}
The tangent operator of a finite-strain elasticity problem is applied tens to
hundreds of times per Newton step, and every application re-evaluates the same
constitutive tangent at every quadrature point of every element, because the
tangent varies within the element and the increment is all that changes between
Krylov iterations.  We eliminate that redundancy by replacing the pulled-back
material tangent with its element-wise $L^2$ projection onto the constants.  The
projection factors the integrand into a per-element tensor, assembled once per
Newton step, and a purely geometric tensor belonging to the reference element,
which is integrated exactly and symbolically at code-generation time and folded
into the emitted kernel as literal constants.  The quadrature loop leaves the
application entirely: it reads a $45$-number store and the increment, and
touches neither geometry, state, nor material parameters.

We give a per-element cost model --- floating-point operations and compulsory
memory traffic --- that places the resulting kernels between $1.0$ and
$6.9$~flop/byte of arithmetic intensity depending on element type, and predicts
that linear tetrahedra are limited by the store bandwidth while quadratic
tetrahedra and trilinear hexahedra are limited by the contraction.  The
generator exploits the second regime by selecting between two contraction
schedules per element on symbolic operation count, which reduces the trilinear
hexahedral kernel from $2732$ to $1801$ operations.

On $1.04\times10^{7}$ degrees of freedom the generated kernels reach
$219.6$~MDOF/s on quadratic tetrahedra against $137.4$ for a hand-optimised
partial-assembly implementation of the same neo-Hookean material, with the
assembly phase also $1.67\times$ faster, and reach parity on trilinear
hexahedra.  Measured against the exact matrix-free application the speed-ups are
$8.1\times$, $2.8\times$ and $2.4\times$ for the three element types, with
break-even below $1.2$ applications per assembly.  Strong scaling on $72$ Arm
Neoverse-V2 cores is $69.4\times$ for the exact application and $73.5\times$ for
the stored one.  The approximation is exact on affine simplices and converges at
$O(h^{1.07})$ elsewhere.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Newton--Krylov solvers for finite-strain elasticity have a lopsided cost
profile.  A Newton step forms one residual and then applies the tangent operator
$n_{\mathrm{it}}$ times inside a Krylov method, where $n_{\mathrm{it}}$ is
typically in the tens to the hundreds.  In a matrix-free implementation each of
those applications evaluates the constitutive tangent at every quadrature point
of every element.  All $n_{\mathrm{it}}$ evaluations produce identical results:
the state is frozen within the step and only the increment changes.

The redundancy is well known and the standard responses are sparse assembly,
which pays once but produces an object that is expensive in memory and
bandwidth-bound to apply, and \emph{partial assembly}, which stores a compact
per-element quantity capturing geometry and state and applies it by a small
tensor contraction.  Partial assembly is routine in high-order matrix-free codes
for linear operators \cite{Orszag1980,DevilleFischerMund2002,KronbichlerKormann2012,Kolev2021,libCEED2021}.
It does not transfer directly to a nonlinear material, because the object one
would store --- the pulled-back tangent $\Sbar$ --- is not constant over the
element.

This paper takes the obvious step and measures the consequences.  We replace
$\Sbar$ by its element average, which factors the integrand into a per-element
store and a reference-element tensor.  The interesting content is not the
approximation, which is elementary, but what becomes possible once it is made:
the reference tensor can be integrated exactly in rational arithmetic before
compilation, its rank structure can be exposed and exploited symbolically, and
the resulting element kernel contains no loads other than the store and the
increment, no branches, and no constants that are not compile-time immediates.
The questions this raises are node-level performance questions: what is the
arithmetic intensity of the resulting kernel, which bound is it against, how does
it vectorise, how does it scale, and does it beat a hand-optimised kernel for
the same material.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item A partial-assembly formulation for nonlinear elasticity by $L^2$ projection
  of the pulled-back tangent (Section~\ref{sec:method}), whose application reads
  only a $45$-number per-element store and the increment.
\item A structural analysis of the reference tensor
  (Section~\ref{sec:refstructure}): it is a Gram matrix, so its rank is the
  dimension of the span of the element's gradient functions, which yields an
  exact rational factorisation and a four-stage contraction schedule.
\item A second contraction schedule and a per-element selection rule on symbolic
  operation count after common-subexpression elimination
  (Section~\ref{sec:schedule}), reducing the trilinear hexahedral kernel by
  $34\%$ and raising its throughput from $100$ to $132$~MDOF/s.
\item A per-element cost model in flops and compulsory bytes
  (Section~\ref{sec:model}), giving arithmetic intensities of $1.0$, $4.9$ and
  $6.9$~flop/byte for TET4, TET10 and HEX8, and predicting distinct bottlenecks
  for the three.
\item A node-level performance study (Section~\ref{sec:results}) at
  $1.04\times10^{7}$ degrees of freedom against hand-optimised kernels for the

  strong scaling to $72$ cores, and a store-precision study.
\item A practical result on storage: the symmetry that halves the store holds for
  energy-based materials and fails for residual-based ones, and assuming it for
  Kelvin--Voigt viscoelasticity produces a different operator with
  $5.3\times10^{-2}$ relative error (Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}

\paragraph{Matrix-free operator application.}  Storing per-quadrature-point geometric
data and applying an operator by tensor contraction originates in
spectral-element methods \cite{Orszag1980,DevilleFischerMund2002} and underpins
modern matrix-free frameworks: the cell-based interface of \texttt{deal.II}
\cite{KronbichlerKormann2012,KronbichlerKormann2019}, MFEM \cite{MFEM2021}, and
libCEED \cite{libCEED2021}, whose ``Q-data'' plays the role of $\Sbar$ here.
The performance of these frameworks rests on sum factorisation, whose asymptotic
advantage appears at polynomial degrees well above those considered in this work
\cite{MelenkGerdesSchwab2001,Kolev2021}.  Our regime is complementary: low to
moderate order, where element tensors are small enough to be manipulated exactly
and symbolically, and where the dominant cost is the repeated evaluation of a
nonlinear constitutive law rather than basis-function contraction.

\paragraph{Nonlinear matrix-free solvers.}  Brown \cite{Brown2010} analyses
matrix-free Newton--Krylov methods for nodal high-order elements in three
dimensions and identifies tangent evaluation as the bottleneck.  Jacobian-free
Newton--Krylov methods \cite{KnollKeyes2004} avoid forming the tangent at all at
the price of a directional-difference approximation; we instead form an
approximate tangent explicitly, which is cheaper to apply repeatedly and whose
error is a property of the discretisation rather than of a difference parameter.
The convergence theory for solvers with inexact Jacobian information is
classical \cite{DemboEisenstatSteihaug1982,EisenstatWalker1996}.

\paragraph{Code generation.}  Automatic generation of element kernels from a
symbolic weak form is established \cite{KirbyLogg2006,UFL2014,Firedrake2016}.
What distinguishes the generator described here is that the projection, the
exact rational integration, the rank factorisation and the schedule selection
are all performed symbolically, and the reference tensor appears in the emitted
code only as folded literal constants --- never as a runtime table.

\paragraph{Reduced precision.}  Storing the assembled tensor at reduced precision
connects to the mixed-precision literature
\cite{HighamMary2022,Abdelfattah2021}.  Our finding
(Section~\ref{sec:precision}) is negative and instructive: \texttt{fp16} does not
pay on either machine tested, because the kernels are not at the bandwidth bound
at the problem sizes where the store would dominate.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Formulation}

Let $K$ be an element with reference cell $\refcell$, map
$\boldsymbol{x}(\xhat)$, Jacobian $J$ and $\mathrm{d}V = \det J\,\mathrm{d}\xhat$,
and let $\{\varphi_p\}_{p=1}^{N}$ be the reference basis.  For a hyperelastic
material with first Piola--Kirchhoff stress $P(F)$ \cite{Ogden1972,BonetWood2008}
the matrix-free tangent action on an increment $h$ is
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{i,p}
  = \int_K S_{ijkl}\bigl(F(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\,
    \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial x_l}\,
    \frac{\partial \varphi_p}{\partial x_j}\;\mathrm{d}V ,
  \qquad
  S_{ijkl} = \frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial F_{kl}} .
  \label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
Pulling the derivatives back with $\partial/\partial x_j = \sum_n J^{-1}_{nj}\,
\partial/\partial \xi_n$ and absorbing the geometry into the tangent,
\begin{equation}
  \Sbar_{ikmn} = \sum_{j,l} S_{ijkl}\, J^{-1}_{nj}\, J^{-1}_{ml}\, \det J ,
  \qquad
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{i,p}
  = \int_{\refcell} \Sbar_{ikmn}(\xhat)\,
    \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial \xi_m}\,
    \frac{\partial \varphi_p}{\partial \xi_n}\;\mathrm{d}\xhat .
  \label{eq:reference}
\end{equation}
Everything under this integral except $\Sbar$ belongs to the reference element.
The quadrature loop exists solely because $\Sbar$ depends on $\xhat$, through $F$.

Replacing $\Sbar$ by its element average
$\Sbar^0 = |\refcell|^{-1}\int_{\refcell}\Sbar$ --- its $L^2$ projection onto the
constants --- removes it from the integral:
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{H} h)_{i,p}
  \;\approx\;
  \sum_{k,m,n} \Sbar^0_{ikmn}\, \sum_j h_{k,j}\, \Wbar_{jmpn} ,
  \qquad
  \Wbar_{jmpn}
  = \int_{\refcell}
    \frac{\partial \varphi_j}{\partial \xi_m}\,
    \frac{\partial \varphi_p}{\partial \xi_n}\;\mathrm{d}\xhat .
  \label{eq:projected}
\end{equation}
The two factors have entirely different lifetimes.  $\Sbar^0$ is a small
per-element tensor computed once per Newton step; $\Wbar$ depends only on the
element type and is integrated exactly, in rational arithmetic, before
compilation.  Algorithm~\ref{alg:split} states the resulting split.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Partial assembly by projection, within one Newton step}
\label{alg:split}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{once per Newton step:}
\ForAll{elements $K$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State evaluate $F$, $S(F)$ and $J$ on $K$; form $\Sbar$ by \eqref{eq:reference}
  \State $\Sbar^0_K \gets$ element average of $\Sbar$ \Comment{$45$ or $81$ numbers}
\EndFor
\Statex
\State \textbf{once per Krylov iteration:}
\ForAll{elements $K$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State gather $h|_K$; contract with $\Sbar^0_K$ and the folded constants of $\Wbar$
  \State scatter the result \Comment{no geometry, state or material read}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Accuracy}

On an affinely mapped simplex with a P1 basis, $J$ and $\nabla h$ are constant,
hence $F$ and $\Sbar$ are constant, hence $\Sbar^0 = \Sbar$ and
\eqref{eq:projected} is an identity: the projection is exact for any
constitutive law, nonlinear or not.  This is elementary but operationally
central, because it gives a configuration in which the approximate kernel must
reproduce the exact one to round-off, independently of every other component of
the implementation.  It is the control for every measurement in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Elsewhere the projection reproduces constants and is therefore first-order
accurate in the element size by the standard Bramble--Hilbert argument
\cite{BrambleHilbert1970,Ciarlet1978}.  The measured rate is $O(h^{1.07})$
(Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

\subsection{What is stored, and when symmetry may be assumed}
\label{sec:symmetry}

The element matrix implied by \eqref{eq:projected} is
$\Sbar^0_{ikmn}\Wbar_{jmpn}$, and since $\Wbar_{jmpn} = \Wbar_{pnjm}$, requiring
it to be symmetric under exchange of the two (component, node) pairs is
equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  \Sbar^0_{ikmn} = \Sbar^0_{kinm} ,
  \label{eq:tangentsym}
\end{equation}
which reduces the store from $81$ to $45$ numbers in three dimensions and from
$16$ to $10$ in two.  The pair that exchanges is $(i,n)$ against $(k,m)$: the
output component with the \emph{test} direction, and the increment component
with the \emph{increment} direction.  Packing on $(i,k)$ against $(m,n)$ instead
is a consistent error, invisible to any test that compares one form of the
construction against another, and detectable only by comparison against
\eqref{eq:exact} integrated independently.

Whether \eqref{eq:tangentsym} holds at all is a property of the formulation.  A
material given by a strain energy has a flux that is a gradient, so its tangent
is a Hessian and the symmetry follows.  A material given directly by a residual
has no such guarantee: its tangent is a Jacobian and need not be symmetric.
Kelvin--Voigt viscoelasticity is a case in point; for the parameters used in
Section~\ref{sec:results} we measure
$\max_{ijkl}|A_{ijkl}-A_{klij}| = 0.25$, so folding it into $45$ components
averages the antisymmetric part away and produces a \emph{different operator},
with $5.3\times10^{-2}$ relative error on TET4 --- an element on which the
projection itself is exact.  The generator therefore decides symmetry per
material, symbolically, and defaults to unsymmetric: storing a full square for a
symmetric tangent wastes bandwidth, whereas storing half of an unsymmetric one
is silently wrong.

\section{Structure of the reference tensor}
\label{sec:refstructure}

Because $\Wbar$ is integrated exactly its entries are rationals, and there are
very few distinct ones: at most ten for any element considered
(Table~\ref{tab:reference}).  On simplices most entries vanish.

The sparsity is a symptom of a stronger property.  Read as a matrix on the pair
index $(j,m)$, $\Wbar$ is by definition the Gram matrix in $L^2(\refcell)$ of the
family $\{\partial\varphi_j/\partial\xi_m\}$, so its rank is the dimension of the
span of that family (Table~\ref{tab:rank}).  A linear simplex is rank one because
its gradients are constants, which is precisely the classical geometric-factor
formulation: one object per element, contracted once.  The rank structure is
therefore not a separate mechanism but the same one carried to elements whose
gradients vary.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
element & order $Nd$ & rank $r$ & span of the gradient functions \\
\midrule
TRI3  &  6 & 1 & constants \\
TET4  & 12 & 1 & constants \\
QUAD4 &  8 & 3 & $1,\ \xi_0,\ \xi_1$ \\
HEX8  & 24 & 7 & $1$ and the six bilinear monomials \\
TET10 & 30 & 4 & $1,\ \xi_0,\ \xi_1,\ \xi_2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Rank of the reference tensor, which equals the dimension of the span of
the element's gradient functions.}
\label{tab:rank}
\end{table}

Since the rank is known and the entries rational, the factorisation
\begin{equation}
  \Wbar = C^{\mathsf T} X C ,
  \qquad C \in \Q^{r \times Nd},
  \qquad X \in \Q^{r \times r},
  \label{eq:factorisation}
\end{equation}
is computed exactly, with no tolerance and no loss, and verified against the
tensor it factors over the rationals.

\section{Kernel synthesis}
\label{sec:schedule}

\subsection{Two contraction schedules}

Substituting \eqref{eq:factorisation} into \eqref{eq:projected} and naming each
intermediate gives a four-stage schedule:
\begin{align}
  P_{kam} &= \sum_j C_{a,(j,m)}\, h_{k,j}
            && \text{compress the increment} \label{eq:stage1}\\
  Y_{ian} &= \sum_{k,m} \Sbar^0_{ikmn}\, P_{kam}
            && \text{apply the tangent} \\
  Q_{ibn} &= \sum_a X_{ab}\, Y_{ian}
            && \text{apply the middle factor} \\
  \text{out}_{ip} &= \sum_{b,n} C_{b,(p,n)}\, Q_{ibn}
            && \text{expand back to nodes} \label{eq:stage4}
\end{align}
Every sum skips its zero coefficients, so a vanishing entry never enters an
expression rather than being cancelled out of one afterwards.  The staging is
load-bearing: expanding \eqref{eq:stage1}--\eqref{eq:stage4} into one expression
per output and letting common-subexpression elimination rediscover the structure
recovers the dense operation count exactly.

Staging is nonetheless not always cheaper.  It must compress the increment and
expand the result, and where the rank is a large fraction of the order those two
stages cost more than the factorisation saves.  The alternative contracts
$\Wbar$ against the increment directly,
\begin{align}
  G_{kmpn} &= \sum_j \Wbar_{jmpn}\, h_{k,j} ,
  &
  \text{out}_{ip} &= \sum_{k,m,n} \Sbar^0_{ikmn}\, G_{kmpn} ,
  \label{eq:direct}
\end{align}
so that $\Sbar^0$ appears once, in a single $27$-term contraction per output,
with nothing to compress or expand.

Which schedule wins is a property of the element, so the generator constructs
both symbolically and emits the cheaper (Table~\ref{tab:ordering}).  On HEX8 the
selection reduces the emitted application from $2732$ to $1801$ operations,
against $1558$ for a hand-written kernel for the same contraction, and raises
measured throughput from $100$ to $132$~MDOF/s (Section~\ref{sec:throughput}).

The comparison must be made \emph{after} elimination.  On the unsimplified
expressions the direct schedule appears inferior on every element, because
elimination is exactly what removes its redundancy; a selection rule using the
pre-elimination count rejects it universally.

\subsection{Constant folding versus runtime tabulation}

The entries of $\Wbar$, $C$ and $X$ are known at generation time and are emitted
as literals.  Tabulating the distinct values at runtime and indexing them --- the
apparently attractive alternative --- compresses a negligible amount of memory
and destroys the arithmetic: the constants cease to be constants, the compiler
can no longer fold them, and every multiplication by a zero entry survives into
the emitted code.  A tabulated TET4 kernel stores a table containing exactly $1$,
$-1$ and $0$, and then multiplies by the zero entry twelve times per kernel; the
nominal ``$97.9\%$ memory saving'' buys $141$ stored numbers that were $0$ and
$\pm1$ at a cost of several hundred operations (Table~\ref{tab:cost}).

\subsection{Vectorisation and register pressure}

Kernels process a block of elements at a time with the lane loop innermost.
Three properties of the staged form matter for that layout.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item[The reference tensor is lane-invariant.]  $C$ and $X$ are compile-time
  rationals, identical for every lane.  Folded, they appear as immediates: no
  load, no broadcast, and multiplication by $\pm1$ or a power of two is
  eliminated.  Behind a runtime table the same values become a lane-independent
  memory reference --- a broadcast load rather than an immediate --- and, more
  damagingly, an opaque value that no longer permits folding.
\item[The store is a unit-stride stream.]  $\Sbar^0$ is held one component-array
  per index in a structure-of-arrays layout, so each of the $45$ components is a
  unit-stride read across the lane loop and vectorises without gather.
\item[The intermediates are lane-width vectors.]  $P$, $Y$ and $Q$ are
  per-element scalars, so each occupies one vector register; all three stage
  sizes are $d^2 r$, so rank governs register pressure
  (Table~\ref{tab:stages}).  On HEX8, $63+63+63$ live intermediates at a lane
  width of eight will not stay in registers and the kernel spills.
\end{description}

\midrule

The two difficulties therefore coincide: on the elements where the projection is
exact the register pressure is lowest, and on HEX8, where it is an
approximation, register pressure is highest --- and HEX8 is also the element on
which the staged schedule loses to the direct one, which needs no intermediates.
Rank truncation, keeping $r' < r$ modes, remains available for elements where
staging still wins, at the cost of a second and larger approximation.

The final stage writes one value per node per component and reuses the
conventional scatter: atomic accumulation on an unordered traversal, plain
accumulation into thread-private scratch on a block-ordered one.

\section{Cost model}
\label{sec:model}

We model the stored application per element.  Let $n_c$ be the number of stored
components, $s_t$ the store precision in bytes, $N$ the node count, $d = 3$, and
$s_i$, $s_r$ the index and vector precisions.  Compulsory traffic per element is
\begin{equation}
  B_e \;=\; \underbrace{n_c s_t}_{\text{store}}
        \;+\; \underbrace{N s_i}_{\text{connectivity}}
        \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{2 N d\, s_r}{\kappa}}_{\text{increment and output}} ,
  \qquad
  \kappa = \frac{N\, n_{\mathrm{elem}}}{n_{\mathrm{nodes}}} ,
  \label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the mean number of elements sharing a node, which amortises the
gathered increment and the scattered output over the elements that reuse them.
The store is streamed once and never reused, so it is charged in full.

For the configuration measured here, $s_t = 4$ (\texttt{fp32} store), $s_i = 4$
(32-bit indices), $s_r = 8$ (\texttt{fp64} vectors), $n_c = 45$; and for the
structured meshes used, $\kappa = 24$, $7.5$ and $8$ for TET4, TET10 and HEX8
respectively.  Taking $W_e$ from the \emph{selected} schedule
(Table~\ref{tab:ordering}) gives the intensities in Table~\ref{tab:intensity}.

The model separates the three elements sharply.  TET4 sits at
$1.0$~flop/byte: it does so little arithmetic that its cost is essentially the
cost of streaming its own store, and it is the element for which reduced store
precision should matter most.  TET10 and HEX8, at $4.9$ and $6.9$~flop/byte,
are on the compute side of any plausible machine balance, and their performance
should be governed by the contraction --- which is what makes the schedule
selection of Section~\ref{sec:schedule} worth performing.

nearly six times the bytes per degree of freedom of the other two, because a
structured tetrahedral mesh has six elements per lattice cell and each carries

\label{tab:perdof}
\end{table}

A consequence worth stating in advance of the measurements: partial assembly is
not uniformly attractive.  Its benefit is the arithmetic it removes, and TET4
has little to remove; its store, by contrast, is the largest per degree of
freedom of the three.  The method should therefore help TET4 least and TET10
most, and Section~\ref{sec:throughput} confirms this ($2.4\times$ against
$8.1\times$ over the exact application).

\section{Implementation}

The scheme is implemented in the code generator of SFEM, an open-source
finite-element library.  From a symbolic material description the generator
derives the flux, differentiates it, performs the pullback, decides symmetry,
integrates $\Wbar$ exactly, computes the factorisation \eqref{eq:factorisation},
selects the schedule by operation count, and emits per element type an
\emph{assembly} kernel consuming geometry and state and producing the store, and
an \emph{application} kernel consuming the store and the increment.  A third
kernel reads a reduced-precision store with a per-element scaling factor applied
to the outputs rather than to the components.

The kernels reach the library's solver stack through three operator methods
whose defaults decline, so existing operators and callers are unaffected: a
query reporting whether the approximate path is available for the current mesh
and element type, an explicit update assembling the store from the current state,
and an application taking the increment alone.  Making the update explicit is
deliberate --- the store remains valid until the next update, which is the reuse
the method exists to exploit, and the application takes no state argument, so it
cannot silently consume a stale one.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Machines.}  \textbf{M1}: Apple M1~Max, $8$ performance and $2$
efficiency cores, Homebrew Clang 20.1.8, OpenMP enabled.  Nominal
double-precision vector peak across the eight performance cores is
approximately $410$~GFLOP/s (four $128$-bit NEON FMA pipes per core at
$\sim$$3.2$~GHz); this figure is derived from the microarchitecture, not
measured.  Nominal package memory bandwidth is $400$~GB/s, of which the CPU
complex can sustain roughly half.  \textbf{Grace}: one node of a GH200 system
(CSCS Alps) with $72$ Arm Neoverse-V2 cores.

\paragraph{Build.}  \texttt{real\_t} is \texttt{double}, \texttt{geom\_t} and
\texttt{metric\_tensor\_t} are \texttt{float}, \texttt{idx\_t} is
\texttt{int32\_t}.  All operators within a comparison are built identically and
run in the same process; figures from different machines or configurations are
never combined in one table.

\paragraph{Timed region.}  The timed region contains the operator application and
nothing else; output initialisation, allocation and correctness checking are
outside it, and each sample averages many repetitions so that no measurement is
charged OpenMP team creation or a cold cache.  This is not pedantry: a serial
output initialisation inside the timed region perturbs the single-thread figure
by $6\%$ ($2.52$ against $2.68$~MDOF/s) and the eight-thread figure by $44\%$
($13.04$ against $18.75$), reporting a speed-up of $5.4\times$ where the true
figure is $7.0\times$.  The distortion grows with thread count, which is the
worst possible shape for a scaling study.

\paragraph{Problem sizes.}  Throughput is reported only at sizes that saturate the
machine.  The comparison against library operators uses
$1.04\times10^{7}$~dof; the scaling study uses $2.07\times10^{5}$~dof.  Every
table states its size, thread count and machine.

\paragraph{Comparison frame.}  Calling a kernel directly with precomputed geometry
and calling it through the library's operator interface are not interchangeable
frames: at $1.70\times10^{5}$~dof the same kernel measures approximately
$5$~MDOF/s in the first and $1.46$ in the second.  Only the second is comparable
with library operators, and all figures below are taken in it.

\paragraph{Accuracy measurements.}  Taken at non-power-of-two resolutions.  On a
mesh whose spacing is a power of two the entries of an \texttt{fp32} store are
exactly representable and the difference from the exact application collapses to
$10^{-14}$, suggesting a far stronger conclusion than the truth
(Table~\ref{tab:precision}).  Convergence rates are measured against a
white-noise increment, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Validation}

The construction is verified at four independent points: the symbolic
integration against four-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature on QUAD4 and HEX8,
entry by entry, to $10^{-16}$; the factorisation against the tensor it factors,
exactly over the rationals; the action against \eqref{eq:exact} integrated
independently on a concrete tetrahedron, for linear elasticity and neo-Hookean
Ogden material; and the generated operator inside the library, driven as a
solver drives it.

Only the third detects an incorrect index convention, and it did: the symmetry
\eqref{eq:tangentsym} was initially implemented as $(i,k)$ against $(m,n)$,
which the first two checks and any comparison of the staged schedule against the
dense form all pass, because they share the error.  This is the practical reason
for stating the index pairing explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}.

TET10 is two orders of magnitude worse than HEX8 at comparable distortion.  Both

projection onto the constants discards.

Measuring the convergence rate requires care in the choice of increment.
Against a smooth increment the TET10 error remains at $1.0\times10^{-2}$ across
an eightfold refinement and appears not to converge.  The cause is in the metric,
not the operator: for a smooth field the assembled result is a discrete second
derivative, so contributions from neighbouring elements cancel and the
denominator of the relative error collapses by an additional order, while the
per-element projection errors carry independent signs and do not cancel.  Against
a white-noise increment the same kernel converges at $O(h^{1.07})$.  At $w=0.4$
the measured sequences are TET10 smooth $1.07\mathrm{e}{-}2 \rightarrow
9.93\mathrm{e}{-}3$ (flat) against random $2.73\mathrm{e}{-}3 \rightarrow
2.95\mathrm{e}{-}4$, and HEX8 smooth $1.16\mathrm{e}{-}3 \rightarrow
2.08\mathrm{e}{-}5$.

\paragraph{Break-even.}  The number of applications per assembly at which the

the hand-optimised operator; the exact matrix-free application is the
appropriate denominator.  Against it, TET10 breaks even at $1.03$ applications
per assembled tangent and TET4 at $1.16$.  A Krylov method performs tens to
hundreds of applications per Newton step, so the assembly is amortised after the
first iteration.

\texttt{fp32} as the operating point.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the method pays.}  It converts state-dependent work into a one-off
assembly plus a state-independent application, so it is favourable exactly when
the ratio of applications to assemblies is high --- Krylov iterations within a
Newton step, repeated smoothing at a fixed state within a multigrid cycle --- and
unfavourable when the state changes every application.  With break-even below
$1.2$, the threshold is essentially always met in a Newton--Krylov context.

\paragraph{Where it is inadmissible.}  The scheme perturbs the operator by $O(h)$.
Used as the operator of record it changes the discrete problem.  Its proper roles
are as the Krylov operator inside an inexact-Newton iteration, where the residual
is still evaluated exactly, and as a preconditioner.  Quantifying the effect on
Newton convergence for a specific forcing-term strategy is the most immediate
open question and is not addressed here.

\paragraph{The remaining performance gap.}  At $18$--$19\%$ of nominal vector peak
the compute-bound elements are not close to the machine.  Three causes are
identifiable and separable: the gather and scatter at element boundaries, which
the cost model charges as bandwidth but which also costs instructions and
inhibits vectorisation at block edges; register spilling on HEX8, where
Table~\ref{tab:stages} exceeds the register file; and the $1801$ against $1558$
operation gap to the hand-written HEX8 contraction, which is a scheduling
question rather than a methodological one.

\paragraph{Element coverage and higher order.}  The two-dimensional kernels are
generated and verified symbolically but have not been executed, pending support
for the required affine geometry cache on triangular and quadrilateral meshes in
the underlying mesh library.  The rank grows with polynomial degree, so at
sufficiently high order the factorisation ceases to pay --- the trend is already
visible at HEX8 in Table~\ref{tab:ordering} --- and sum factorisation becomes the
appropriate technique instead.  Determining the crossover degree is future work.

\section{Conclusions}

Projecting the pulled-back material tangent onto element-wise constants removes
the quadrature loop from the matrix-free application of a nonlinear elasticity
operator, leaving an application that reads a $45$-number store and the
increment and nothing else.  The reference tensor that remains is a Gram matrix
whose rank is the dimension of the span of the element's gradient functions; it
is factored exactly in rational arithmetic and folded into the emitted kernel as
literal constants.

A per-element cost model places the resulting kernels at $1.0$, $4.9$ and
$6.9$~flop/byte for TET4, TET10 and HEX8, predicts that the first is limited by
its own store and the other two by the contraction, and correctly orders the
measured speed-ups over the exact matrix-free application ($2.4\times$,
$8.1\times$, $2.8\times$).  At $1.04\times10^{7}$~dof on eight cores the
generated TET10 kernel is $1.60\times$ faster than a hand-optimised
partial-assembly implementation of the same material, with a $1.67\times$ faster
assembly, and HEX8 is at parity; strong scaling on $72$ Arm cores is $69.4\times$
and $73.5\times$.  Reduced-precision storage does not pay at the problem sizes
measured, for reasons the cost model accounts for.

Three directions follow.  The first is the inexact-Newton analysis: with an
$O(h)$ Jacobian perturbation, what forcing-term strategy retains fast
convergence, and how does the resulting iteration count trade against the
per-application saving?  The second is closing the gap to vector peak, where the
gather--scatter and HEX8's register pressure are the identified causes.  The
third is rank truncation: the modes are explicit, and discarding the smallest is
a controlled second approximation.
