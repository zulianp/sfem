SFEM Codegen Architecture

Every plan the planning layer produces now has a reader, and the decisions left in emission are down from 428 to 200 under a shrink-only ratchet. Two things changed character since the last pass. Byte-identity stopped being the universal gate, because the first changes that are meant to alter the output landed — closed-form simplex kernels, a collapsed public ABI, a narrowed scope. And the shipped tree turned out to have gone six weeks without being regenerated, which is now gated, and which had shipped a Laplacian whose block scatter indexed a 4×4 element matrix as 12×12.

Package python/codegen/framework Branch codegen-layering, 178 commits from 060684ff, at 700cef824 Out of scope the mlir/ subtree Date 2026-09-08 (third pass)

This document separates two things that the previous analysis deliberately ran together: what the architecture requires of each layer, and what each layer currently is. They are drawn against the same eight rows so the difference can be pointed at rather than described. Everything after that is the list of decisions still open, with the cost of each and what it blocks.

Where things stand

Structure is done: every import in the framework points up the lowering order, zero violations, enforced by a test that fails on any new one. The specification layer has no outgoing edges at all. Nothing architectural lives outside the framework.

Data flow is no longer the headline gap. Every structural plan has a reader — the count of unread plans went 13 → 0, and three of the thirteen stopped existing when the matrix formats were cut. Direction is the gap that remains: L3 describes the kernel, L6 prints it, and L6 still originates 200 of the decisions, 192 of them in two modules.

What the last stretch established is that the ratchet is worth more than the convenient move. Twice now a change that worked, compiled and passed every functional test was rejected because it pushed the decision count up by one: reading plan.emits_packed inside an emitter is still emission choosing what to emit. Both times the accepted form was a sequence the emitter iterates — empty, and it emits nothing — which is the shape the rest of this work should take.

Put plainly: the boxes are in the right places, the arrows point the right way, and the plans are read — but the emitter still decides most of what the plans describe. Every open point in Part 3 is either that problem, a consequence of it, or a piece of scope deliberately set down while it is fixed.

The shape is right; the direction of data flow is not. L3 computes the structure, L4 can represent it, L6 prints it — and L6 still originates it. That has not changed, and it is the same defect that keeps being fixed one narrow place at a time: the scheduler computed hoist_scope and the emitters re-derived the loop nest; the driver selected a target and the emitters built their own; the geometry policy was a plan and four sites in the energy emitter each spelled it again. Each instance closes. The general case is open.

What did change is the gate. For most of this branch every step was proved by byte-identity across the generated tree, which settles correctness and performance at once and is why so much could be moved so fast. That gate has now been spent: closed-form simplex kernels, a collapsed ABI and a narrowed scope all change the output on purpose. What replaces it is answer-level — kernels driven on real meshes and digested, with parity between variants and a throughput comparison — and it is weaker, slower and the thing to keep investing in. The check-tree gate is the other half of the same lesson: the framework was six weeks ahead of the tree the build compiles, and nothing was comparing them.

Open points

Twenty-five, ordered by what unblocks what. Four are new since the last pass, and two of those are already closed — they are kept because both are findings rather than tasks, and a closed finding that is deleted gets rediscovered. OP 24
The shipped tree had stopped being regenerated, and nothing was checking closed — check-tree gates it now

The finding. frontend/ops/generated/ is globbed into libsfem by CMakeLists.txt and is never regenerated by the build, so it is what actually ships. Six weeks of generator work had not reached the binary: of the 374 paths the tree shared with the snapshot manifest, 195 were byte-identical and 179 were not. Both existing gates — verify and check — ask whether the generator still agrees with a record of itself, which cannot detect a tree that simply stopped being regenerated.

What it had been hiding. A scalar Laplacian written as an energy whose BSR scatter declared N_FIELD_COMPONENTS = dim — right for a displacement, which was every energy material the framework had, and wrong for a scalar field. The scatter indexed a 4×4 element matrix as if it were 12×12: out of bounds by nine times its own size, producing values around 1e+210 and garbage that moved between CRS and BSR when the thread count changed. Four separate sites in the energy wrapper and scatter emitters spelled the component count as dim. scalar_potential, the only other scalar material, had the same defect.

The gate. codegen_snapshot check-tree regenerates into a temporary directory and compares against the shipped tree, exiting non-zero with a unified diff and the command that fixes it. Six paths are exempt and listed: the four .cuh headers, which only generators.cuda writes and only under SFEM_GENERATE_CUDA=1, and the registration unit, which is manifest-driven and hand-edited. It was verified in both directions — it passes on the tree as committed and fails, naming the file, when one comment is appended to a generated source. A test pins the exemption list at six root-level files, because an entry quietly added there would hide exactly the class of drift the gate exists to catch.

The general lesson, which is the reason this stays on the list. Every other gate on this branch compares the generator against a record of the generator. This is the only one that compares it against the artifact a user receives, and it is the one that found a wrong answer. Any future gate should be asked which of the two it is.

OP 22
Partial assembly is a generated kernel variant, on the C ABI built and measured; one material declares it

The framework now generates a second form of the Jacobian action alongside the exact matrix-free one: precompute the compressed element tangent, store it, and have the apply read it instead of the Jacobian and the state. It is reached through inexact_apply on the material and --inexact-apply/--no-inexact-apply on the generator, it appears on the public C ABI as its own entry points, and Op declares inexact_supported and inexact_update so a solver can ask for it.

It is measured rather than argued. On Neo-Hookean Ogden at 1.04×107 dof, eight threads of an M1, the stored variant reaches 219.6 MDOF/s on quadratic tetrahedra against 137.4 for a hand-optimised reference, and HEX8 is at parity. Break-even is below 1.2 applications per assembly. On one node of a GH200 (72 Arm Neoverse-V2 cores, CSCS Alps) at 2.07×105 dof it scales 73.5× against the exact matrix-free path's 69.4× — essentially linear, which refuted an earlier reading that attributed a plateau to the scatter.

Two corrections the work produced, both worth keeping. A projected variant was measured first and is slower on every element it can reach, so it was dropped rather than kept as an option. And a TET10 "non-convergence" attributed to the approximation turned out to be the metric — the approximation was fine and the measurement was not.

What is open. One material declares it. Whether the split is a per-element choice the plan layer should make for every material, rather than a flag set on one, is the question this entry holds; it is the same shape as every other axis in Part 2 that emission still decides.

OP 20
Energy and residual must lower to one form algebra the vocabulary is shared; the contraction still is not

The requirement. Both formulations lower to the same three objects — 0-form (energy, or a residual-based merit), 1-form (gradient, or the negated residual), 2-form (Hessian, or the Jacobian) — and nothing below the form layer distinguishes them.

The algebra already exists and stops at emission. symbolic/forms.py has FormOrder, StandardFormName, and FormCollection.form(order) / blocks_for(order). FormOrder is used 33 times in symbolic, 23 in plans, 18 in package, 20 in pipeline — and 0 times in emitters. The split below the form layer is not a flag the emitters test; it is the module boundary itself.

Only seven function names are shared between them, and stripping the formulation word from the names adds none. They are parallel implementations of the same concepts rather than copies, which is why every capability is built twice and can diverge — and between them they hold 231 of the 233 decisions left in emission. The fork is one branch, in pipeline/driver.py, choosing an energy or a residual codegen unit on FormKind.

Phase 0 measured which representation is actually load-bearing, and it is not the forms. The parallel residual state — residual_fields, residual_expressions, jacobian_action_blocks — has 49 consumers across eight files. The form-order accessors have 19 across five. So re-keying is not a rename: it is making the form blocks become the representation everything reads, in place of the one they read now.

The obstacle is smaller than this entry first claimed, and the first draft of it was wrong twice. It reported that energy formulations "populate no block metadata at all" and treated that as a gap phase 1 must fill, and it described the keying difference as a mismatch that forces a choice between two representations. Neither survives contact with the API.

  • The energy path declares its structure at the front end, and that is where it belongs. system.add_energy("", energy, fields=(u,), variables=(F,)) supplies the field and the group of variables the energy differentiates through, and _validate_energy_variable_groups enforces one variable group per field. Linear elasticity arrives as fields=[('u', 3)] with variables=F[0..8]. Empty blocks_for() is not missing information; the information came in through the API.
  • Both kinds already key FormCollection.fields the same way — by assembled field. Laplace ['u'], Stokes ['u', 'p'], linear elasticity ['u']. There is no disagreement at that level.
  • And the per-component expansion loses nothing. The residual lowering builds it through builder.add_field(component_name, field_name=field.name, component=i, components=n), so Stokes's lowered fields report u0 → parent u, component 0 of 3. Both keyings are projections of one object.

So the two representations are reconcilable rather than competing, and phase 1 is not "choose a keying". It is: carry the per-component expansion the residual lowering already builds into the form blocks, and derive the same for energy from the fields and variables the API already supplies. The information exists on both sides today.

One correction to the plan this phase was written from. It said the residual 0-form does not exist, on the strength of FormMetadata(FormOrder.ZERO) being appended with no blocks. The metadata has no blocks, but the form does have an expression: laplace's 0-form is κ²(∇u·∇v)²/2, which is already the shape of a merit. What is missing is the block metadata and a consumer, not the mathematics — so phase 2 is smaller than planned. Worth noting that this expression is the square of the weak-form integrand, test function included, rather than of the assembled residual vector; whether that is the intended merit is a question for whoever specifies it.

What phase 0 leaves behind. tests/test_form_keying_baseline.py records, for every material and dimension, which orders exist, what each order's blocks are keyed by, and what the lowered fields are called. Phase 1 then arrives as a diff against that file rather than as an assertion that nothing broke. It deliberately does not assert that the 1-form is the derivative of the 0-form: on the residual path the 0-form is a merit and the 1-form is the negated residual, and the two are not related that way.

Phase 1, first half: the residual side now reaches its per-component blocks through the form layer. FormCollection.component_blocks_for(order) returns them — Stokes reports u0, u1, u2, p at 1-form order where blocks_for reports u and p, and sixteen blocks at 2-form order where blocks_for reports three. Nothing new is computed; the lowering already built all of it under residual-specific names. plans/residual_model.py is the first consumer moved onto the accessor, and all 505 files stay byte-identical.

One thing the attempt got wrong first, worth keeping. The blocks were re-wrapped as FormBlock to make the return type uniform — which renames them, because FormBlock.name is derived as form_2_<row>_<column> while the lowering's blocks carry a name that already appears in generated kernels. The accessor hands back the lowering's own objects instead. Re-exposing is the job; renaming is not, and a phase that must be byte-identical cannot afford it.

Phase 1b stopped, and the reason changes the plan. The energy side has nothing to re-expose, so the work was to derive per-component blocks from the declared fields and variables. Following dF/du to see what that costs turned up something better: the two paths do not carry the same object at all.

A residual 1-form is a weak form: it names its test function and the contraction is finished before emission sees it. An energy 1-form is dE/dF, a strong quantity, contracted against the test functions afterwards by _append_transformed_loperand_lines and tensor_test — and that contraction is sum-factorised, working through one-dimensional shape_1d and grad_1d operators with integer_root(N_QP) per dimension. Counting it in the generated tree: the two energy materials reach tensor_test, the two residual materials reach it zero times.

So the split is not energy against residual. It is strong-contracted against weak-contracted, and the front end a material happens to be written in silently decides which it gets. Making the energy path produce weak forms — the obvious way to unify — would take sum factorisation away from the only materials that have it, on exactly the tensor-product elements it exists for. That is the trade this codebase does not make.

It also explains something OP 18 recorded without accounting for. Laplace, the simplest and most performance-critical operator here, is written as a residual and therefore gets no sum factorisation, which is the context for its 389 temporaries and for the hand-written low-order fast path someone wrote to compensate.

OP 19
The mesh geometry a kernel takes is now the plan's decision done — and extended to the variant cross-product

Choosing the next step by measurement rather than by feel: of the 239 decisions left in emission, 231 are in the two big emitters, and they split cleanly by what they read. The residual emitter's 126 are dominated by dependencies (74), testing exactly the flags the plan layer already answers — current, direction, previous, uses_adjugate. Which geometry buffers cross a mesh kernel's boundary — the symmetric gradient metric, or the Jacobian adjugate and determinant — was spelled by hand at twenty-five sites in the residual emitter, four in the energy one, and once more in the wrapper, each with its own if chain over the same flags. plans/geometry_quantities.py now states it once, and both the signatures and the calls are spelled from it.

This is the decision that had already gone wrong. The wrapper's copy spelled the adjugate unconditionally, so laplace generated for TRI3 or TET4 alone handed five geometry arguments to a kernel taking three and would not compile — OP 16's third defect. Fixing that made the instance go away; this makes the class go away, and a test pins it in the shape OP 3 established: the names the plan produces must appear in the emitted kernel, and changing the plan must change what is emitted, because the weaker assertion alone passes for an emitter that consults nothing.

All 505 files byte-identical at every step, so nothing about the generated code moved — only where the decision lives. The wrapper also stops recomputing dim * (dim + 1) / 2 for itself and reads the plan's count, which is the arithmetic both sides previously agreed on only by coincidence.

OP 18
Low-order Laplacian and elasticity want CSE expanded, and one of them already has it — as frozen text built for the Laplacian shape; elasticity is the remaining half

The requirement is that the simplest and most important kernels — Laplacian and linear elasticity on low-order elements — expand their common subexpressions rather than sharing them, so the emitted code is small and the register footprint minimal. A shared subexpression is a value live in a register from its definition to its last use; for a low-order element the expression it names is tiny and cheap to recompute, so naming it buys almost no arithmetic and lengthens live ranges across a loop the compiler is trying to vectorise. This is the register-pressure mechanism OP 15 measured, pointing the other way.

Read the last row against the first. The Laplacian on TET4 carries more than twice the temporaries of a hyperelastic material on the same element — 389 against 186 — which is the wrong way round for the simplest operator in the framework, and is the measurement that makes the case.

The low-order Laplacian already has the kernel this asks for. There is a fast path that computes a TET4 element with six temporaries and four outputs, and a TRI3 element with three, straight from the metric components:

const scalar_t x0 = fff0 + fff1 + fff2;
const scalar_t x3 = fff1 * u0;
const scalar_t e0 = u0 * x0 + u0 * x1 + u0 * x2 - u1 * x0 - u2 * x1 - u3 * x2;
const scalar_t e1 = -fff0 * u0 + fff0 * u1 + fff1 * u2 + fff2 * u3 - x3 - x4;

It is hand-written C, as literal strings inside the emitter — 28 such lines across emitters/ — selected by a six-condition structural test that is also in the emitter: cached affine metric, one field, one quadrature point, n_shape == dim + 1, no previous state, and a matching stream group. Every one of those conditions is a property of the lowered form, which is to say a planning decision sitting in emission; and the kernel it selects is not derived from the form at all, it is transcribed.

Linear elasticity gets none of it. The gate tests for a Laplacian-shaped form, so elasticity falls through to the general path and its 284 temporaries. The technique is frozen exactly where someone wrote it out, which is the cost of holding it as text rather than as a policy.

So the work is to make it a plan. Expansion becomes a per-kernel decision the plan layer states — the structural test moves there, where it belongs, and gains the element-order and material conditions elasticity needs. Kernels validate the matrix graph per element removed from every kernel — not yet re-established

Raised by Patrick, not found by this analysis. The generated Hessian assembly checks that the sparsity pattern contains every entry it needs while assembling. Each element's _scatter_bsr walks N_SHAPE × N_SHAPE candidate entries and tests each one with a three-condition branch, keeps a valid_block_graph flag, and calls std::fprintf(stderr, …) from inside an OpenMP parallel region on failure. The caller folds the status per element into invalid_matrix_graph and reduces it across threads with reduction(|:invalid_matrix_graph).

Graph validity is a property of the mesh and the sparsity pattern together — invariant across every element and across the whole assembly call. Establishing it once costs O(nnz); re-testing it inside the loop costs O(elements × N_SHAPE²), which is 100 branches per element for TET10 and far more for the higher-order hexes. Kernels must be correct, but preconditions belong to the caller: a kernel should be lean, and the priority inside an element loop is throughput.

Two facts shape the work. The check is fused into the sparsity search rather than sitting beside it — ks[j] is the search result and the branch validates it in the same pass — so removal keeps the search and drops the test, the flag and the I/O, rather than deleting a loop. And the CRS form carries the same pattern but is not currently emitted for any shipped material, so the real removal is smaller than the emitter source suggests.

Note that the reduction clause plumbed through parallel_for_pragma in OP 2 exists to reproduce this check byte-for-byte. That was the right transitional move under a byte-identity gate, and it is not settled design: if the check is hoisted, the clause and its target API go with it.

Done for BSR, the format vector problems assemble into. What is left is the locate loop, which was never validation: ks[j] is the search result the scatter needs either way, and the check had been fused into the search rather than sitting beside it. The function can no longer fail, so it returns void and its caller folds no status — the arrangement _scatter_block_diag_sym always had.

Answers were bit-identical across all 36 kernels in both runs, so the analysis and the transformation are sound; they simply cost more than they save.

That is the register-pressure effect this entry predicted, now observed: moving scalar work out of a #pragma omp simd loop lengthens live ranges across it. LivenessState already models exactly this, which is the argument for a pass that consults it rather than one that hoists whatever it can.

Open, not closed, and deprioritised. Two of the three largest shares are still unmeasured — poro-elasticity and Stokes are Taylor–Hood on tetrahedra and want a TET10 grid the harness does not build. Given two negative results and an understood mechanism, the expected value is low; but the shares are the largest, so the item stays with its evidence attached. Anything that revisits it should hoist selectively under the liveness model rather than uniformly, and clear the ±2% floor on a machine that is not also full.

One thing the scope cut changed about this entry. Four of the six rows in the table above — poro_elasticity, stokes, modified_mooney_rivlin and two of the five hyperelastic materials — are no longer generated by default. The invariant work was concentrated almost entirely in the coupled and mixed-order simplex kernels, and most of those left the tree with OP 23. What ships today is closer to the zero-opportunity end of that table than to the 15% end, so the measurement that would decide this should be re-taken against the materials that actually ship before any more effort goes into it.

OP 14
The gate for changes that are meant to alter the output 724 measurements; it is now the primary gate

Byte-identity has carried every step of this branch, and it is the strongest statement available while it holds. It is also unavailable for everything left: OP 10 hoists a check out of the kernels, OP 11 deletes dead declarations, OP 12 changes a scratch slot, OP 13 reconciles a name, and every loop-structure pass the IR is supposed to hold rewrites the text by design. For all of those, byte-identity is not the proof — it is the thing that must break.

tools/reproducibility.py is the gate that replaces it, asking the only question that survives an intended output change: given the same inputs, does the kernel still produce the same numbers? It generates each material, compiles the real entry points, drives every one it can bind over a Cartesian grid, and compares an l1/l2 digest per kernel against a committed baseline. --record rewrites that baseline, and the diff it produces is the reviewable statement of exactly which kernels moved.

Nothing in it knows any physics. The generated manifest lists every C ABI entry point with its full declaration, and the parameter names in those declarations are the ABI plans/streams.py defines — nelements, elements, g_jacobian_adjugate<i>, <role>_stride, <field>_direction, <field>_out. Binding a call is reading names, so a material added tomorrow is driven without touching the tool. That the boundary work of this session made those names a single definition is what makes this possible at all.

It carries a second check for free. The grid is affine, so a kernel pair differing only in geometry mode must agree — 24 such pairs, asserted rather than assumed. A digest alone would record a broken adjugate routing as the new correct answer; the parity check will not.

What it does not cover, stated rather than hidden. Packed variants want a mesh partition, matrix assembly wants a sparsity pattern, and the diagnostics entry points have no numerical output at all — each is skipped with its reason printed. Three materials are out of reach: poro-elasticity and Stokes are Taylor–Hood and want an element topology this grid does not build, and two-phase flow's per-form operators each define the same element-generic entry points, so they cannot be linked into one driver. That last one is a fact about the generated code worth its own look.

It has taken over. The baseline is 724 kernel measurements over 366 distinct kernels in six buckets, and three of those buckets — TET4, TRI3 and QUAD4 — did not exist at the last pass. It is what carried the closed-form simplex kernels, the ABI collapse and the matrix-graph removal, none of which byte-identity could have carried.

One thing to fix, small and worth naming. The recorded baseline still holds 239 records for five materials that OP 23 removed from default generation. They are history, not coverage, and the file does not currently say which is which — so the gate's headline number overstates what it actually re-checks.

OP 13
Assembly plans named streams their kernels do not have resolved — the two wrong plans were deleted with their formats

Connecting the last four assembly plans found that two of them were describing a kernel that is not the one being generated. DIAAssemblyPlan.diagonal_offsets said "diagonal_offsets"; every DIA scatter reads a parameter called diag_offsets. COOAssemblyPlan said "rowidx" and "colidx"; the kernels write rows and cols. Nothing was wrong with the generated code — the plans had simply drifted, which they were free to do because no emitter read them. A plan nobody consults is free to be wrong about the thing it exists to define, and that is the strongest argument yet for connecting the rest.

The names could not simply be corrected: they are also the diagnostics index_policy, which is emitted into generated metadata and read by scripts/matrix_format_benchmark_report.py. So each plan keeps its descriptor and carries the C name alongside it — diagonal_offset_stream, row_stream, column_stream — and reconciling the two is a deliberate output change, batched with OP 11 and OP 12 rather than smuggled into a byte-identical step.

A third mismatch surfaced with them: COOAssemblyPlan has no reduction_policy at all. Its accumulation_policy is emit_triplets and its reduction_phase is non_hot_setup_phase, which describe the triplet scatter — but there are two COO kernels, and the other one locates its entry in a sorted graph and accumulates atomically in the hot loop. One plan, two kernels, and it describes one of them.

What is connected, and what is not. Each scatter now takes its stream names and reduction policy from its plan, and test_assembly_plans_name_the_kernels proves the plan is read rather than coincidentally matched, by renaming a stream and requiring the kernel to follow. What that does not yet give is a plan that drives the ABI: the same names appear as literals at roughly a hundred further sites — the mesh-operator signatures and the calls that pass the buffers in, fourteen for rowptr alone — so a rename reaches the scatter's parameters and not its callers, and the result would not compile. The docstrings on the CRS and BSR connections claimed otherwise and have been corrected.

Resolved by deletion rather than by reconciliation. The two plans found to be describing kernels that do not exist were DIAAssemblyPlan and COOAssemblyPlan, and both went with their formats in OP 23 — along with the third mismatch, COO's one plan for two kernels. Nothing was renamed and nothing was reconciled; the drifted descriptions simply stopped being shipped.

What survives is the finding, and it is the important part. A plan nobody consults is free to be wrong about the thing it exists to define, and two of the four were. That is the argument for connecting the rest, and it is why the three surviving assembly plans are now driven and pinned by test_assembly_plans_name_the_kernels, which renames a stream and requires the kernel to follow. The unfinished half also survives: the same names appear as literals at roughly a hundred further sites — mesh-operator signatures and the calls that pass the buffers in — so a rename reaches the scatter's parameters and not its callers, and the result would not compile.
